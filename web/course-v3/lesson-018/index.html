Hardware / 01

Choose what to measure and report

Look closely

A cold-store display warns that the room is warm. Has the system cooled it?

Compare a temperature display with a system that also switches on refrigeration. Both receive measurements; identify the extra path that can change the room temperature.

Step 1 / 5

Identify the physical quantity

A sensor responds to a physical quantity and supplies a corresponding signal. A temperature sensor measures the cold-store temperature. A pressure sensor can measure tyre pressure; a calibrated pressure pad can also provide information about an applied load, such as a vehicle’s weight. Choose by the required measurement. A motor run time is not a pressure reading.

Step 2 / 5

Add infra-red and sound examples

An infra-red receiver detects infra-red radiation, such as a remote-control signal or a directed beam. In a beam alarm, an object interrupts the received beam and the signal changes. A sound sensor detects sound or its level, allowing a workshop noise monitor to compare readings with a limit.

Step 3 / 5

Turn measurements into a report

A monitoring system gathers measurements and may record them, display them or produce a warning. An analogue sensor signal needs conversion before digital processing; a sensor module that already provides digital readings does not need a second external ADC for the same signal. The processor interprets the data according to its program.

Step 4 / 5

Distinguish reporting from regulation

A high-temperature alert tells a person about the condition. It does not itself cool the store. A control system sends an output that changes the process, such as switching on refrigeration. Monitoring and control can be present together; describe what the output actually does to the measured condition.

Step 5 / 5

Check the purpose of an output

A warning lamp or sounder is an output, but its presence alone does not prove automatic regulation of temperature or noise. Ask whether the action changes the process being controlled. The system can monitor a condition and alert a person who then takes action, without automatically making that correction itself.

Explain it yourself · Teacher-written check

A classroom sound sensor displays a high reading and lights a warning lamp. Explain why this is not automatically a closed-loop noise-reduction system.

Check your reasoning

It measures and reports the noise. The lamp does not itself reduce the noise, and no automatic corrective action with returned noise measurements has been specified.

Hardware / 02

Connect a measurement to an actuator

Look closely

Which part measures, which part decides, and which part changes the environment?

Trace the reading, comparison and output paths in the diagram. Suppose the rule is to heat below 20 °C and stop at or above 20 °C. For a reading of 18 °C, which part measures, which part decides and which part acts? Later, test this sequence in Explore.

Step 1 / 5

Send the measured condition to the processor

The sensor measures the physical condition and supplies a corresponding signal. Convert an analogue signal into digital data if necessary. The processor receives the reading; it does not directly know the actual temperature or pressure independently of the measurement supplied to it.

Step 2 / 5

Apply an explicit rule

Consider a heater that is on below 20 °C and off at or above 20 °C. At a measured 18 °C the processor selects ON; at 20 °C it selects OFF. The stored rule, including its boundary, determines the decision. The sensor supplies a reading rather than deciding whether heating is appropriate.

Step 3 / 5

Operate the actuator through a suitable interface

The output command reaches driving circuitry that operates the actuator. An actuator produces a physical action: a heater releases heat, a motor turns a pump or a valve changes flow. A low-power processor output does not itself supply the required motor or heater power.

Step 4 / 5

Choose signal conversion when the interface needs it

A digital switching command can operate a relay or motor driver without a DAC. An actuator interface requiring an analogue command may need digital-to-analogue conversion. Identify the required signal and interface instead of inserting an ADC and DAC automatically into every diagram.

Step 5 / 5

Separate a command from a result

A command to heat is information sent by the controller. An actual temperature rise is a physical result. A disconnected heater can receive an ON command at its controller while producing no heat. To find out what really happened, the system needs a new measurement.

Explain it yourself · Teacher-written check

The controller sends an ON command, but the pump motor is disconnected. What should a working pressure sensor report next?

Check your reasoning

It should report the actual pressure, which may remain unchanged. The measurement must not simply copy the target pressure or assume that the ON command caused pumping.

Hardware / 03

Measure the result and close the loop

Look closely

Why must the controller measure again after switching the heater on?

Follow the return path from the measured room temperature to the controller. Imagine that an ON command has been sent but a broken heater produces no heat. What should a working sensor report next? Later, use Explore to compare the command with the measured outcome.

Step 1 / 5

Return information about the actual condition

Feedback is information about the outcome or current state returned to the controller. A new temperature measurement tells it what happened after the earlier output. The reading may be unchanged if the heater failed. That unchanged measurement is still useful feedback when it accurately represents the room.

Step 2 / 5

Decide again using the stated rule

For the rule ON below 20 °C and OFF at or above 20 °C, readings of 18, 19 and 20 produce ON, ON and OFF. Further measurements matter after switch-off: an open door can cool the room, and the controller may need to start heating again. The measurement–comparison–action cycle continues.

Step 3 / 5

Distinguish feedback from timing alone

A timer can run a heater for two minutes without measuring the room afterwards. This is an open-loop control action: it acts, but cannot adjust from the measured outcome. Closed-loop control uses returned measurements to guide further action and can respond to changes that a fixed time cannot reveal.

Step 4 / 5

Read a rule with two thresholds

Some controllers turn ON below 18 °C, turn OFF at or above 20 °C, and keep the previous state between those limits. Starting OFF, readings 17, 19, 20, 19 produce ON, ON, OFF, OFF. The same 19 °C reading can have different outputs because the stated rule also depends on the previous state.

Step 5 / 5

Use feedback carefully when diagnosing faults

A working sensor with a failed heater may keep reporting an actually cold room. A sensor stuck at 17 °C may report cold even as the room becomes hot, so the controller could keep heating. Feedback is valuable evidence, but a faulty sensor can make it unreliable; feedback alone does not repair a failed component.

Explain it yourself · Teacher-written check

Under the two-threshold rule, why is the heater ON at one 19 °C reading and OFF at a later 19 °C reading?

Check your reasoning

Both readings lie in the band that retains the previous state. The first can follow heating from below 18 °C; the later one can follow switch-off at 20 °C. The prior state and the rule determine the result.

Apply the idea · Teacher-written practice
Question 1 · Command word: State4 teacher marks

State the heater states for readings 17, 19, 20, 19, 18, 17 °C. It starts OFF, turns ON below 18 °C, turns OFF at or above 20 °C, and retains its previous state between those thresholds. Explain the two different outcomes at 19 °C.

S3.08.A03
Answer and checking points — teacher-written
  1. At 17 °C the heater changes from OFF to ON.
  2. The first 19 °C retains ON; 20 °C changes it to OFF.
  3. The later 19 °C and 18 °C retain OFF, then the final 17 °C changes it to ON.
  4. The complete sequence is ON, ON, OFF, OFF, OFF, ON; 19 °C retains a different previous state on the two visits.

Common error: The middle band has no single output independent of history.

Question 2 · Command word: Explain4 teacher marks

Explain the difference between a pump whose pressure sensor is stuck below the target and a pump whose motor fails while its pressure sensor remains accurate. The controller pumps whenever measured pressure is below the target.

S3.08.A03
Answer and checking points — teacher-written
  1. The stuck sensor keeps reporting low pressure even if actual pressure rises.
  2. The controller may therefore keep pumping beyond the intended actual target.
  3. With a failed motor and accurate sensor, the low measurement can correctly reveal that pressure has not risen.
  4. Feedback supplies evidence of the outcome, but does not by itself repair a failed sensor or actuator.

Common error: Distinguish an inaccurate reading from an accurate report of unsuccessful action.

1. Classify a bridge warning system

Cambridge 9618/12 · October/November 2024 · 9(a), 9(b) · 4 marks

Selected parts: 9(a) [2]; 9(b) [2].

Hint — reading the question

For each sensor, give its use in this particular system. In (b), explain whether the system itself changes the situation being measured; a warning can still require the driver to act.

Reasoning and model answer — teacher-written
SensorUse
Infrared sensorChecks the vehicle’s height.
Pressure sensorChecks the vehicle’s weight.

9(b): This is a monitoring system. Turning on the warning sign informs the driver; it does not itself change the vehicle’s height or weight or feed a corrective change back to those sensor inputs. The system has no actuator that automatically stops the vehicle.

Official mark scheme
Why this earns marks — teacher explanation

Part (a) gives one mark for each suitable sensor-and-use pair. In (b), identification alone earns no mark: the two marks are for correct justification, such as the indicator-only output and absence of corrective feedback.

Common mistakes — teacher analysis

Do not award yourself a mark just for writing “monitoring”. The human driver’s response is not an automatic actuator in the specified warning system.

Original question · Cambridge 9618/12

2. Classify an automatic door system

Cambridge 9618/12 · October/November 2025 · 11 · 3 marks

Selected parts: 11 [3].

Hint — reading the question

Explain what changes physically, how this affects subsequent sensing, and why the system can respond without a person operating a switch.

Reasoning and model answer — teacher-written

It is a control system. An actuator opens or closes the doors. Opening the doors allows people to move through, changing whether a person is detected within two metres. The system then responds autonomously to the new sensor input and closes the doors when nobody remains within that distance.

Official mark scheme
Why this earns marks — teacher explanation

The label “control system” earns no mark here. The three marks concern the actuator, the effect of opening on subsequent sensed input, and autonomous action using feedback.

Common mistakes — teacher analysis

Giving only “uses a sensor” does not distinguish control from monitoring. Include the door’s physical action and the next input change.

Compare a monitoring system with a control system.

Answer and checking points — teacher-written

  2. Monitoring records, displays or alerts about measured data.

Question 2 · Command word: Explain4 teacher marks

Explain why feedback is important in a closed-loop temperature-control system.

S3.08.A03
Answer and checking points — teacher-written
  1. A new sensor reading reports the actual temperature after the actuator operates.
  2. The controller compares the new reading with the target.
  3. The output can be continued, adjusted or stopped according to the remaining difference.
  4. Disturbances or incomplete changes can be detected and corrected.

Common error: Feedback is the new sensor information returned to the controller, not the output sent to the actuator.

Question 3 · Command word: Explain5 teacher marks

Explain how a controller should respond to these temperature readings when it switches a heater on below 18 °C and off at or above 20 °C: 17 °C, 19 °C, 20 °C. Between the thresholds it retains the previous state. Identify which readings provide feedback after the heater starts.

S3.08.A01S3.08.A02S3.08.A03S3.09.A01S3.09.A02
Answer and checking points — teacher-written
  1. A temperature sensor provides the readings for the controller's stored rule.
  2. At 17 °C the controller switches the heater actuator on.
  3. At 19 °C it keeps the heater on because the upper switching threshold has not been reached.
