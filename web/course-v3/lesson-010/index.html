Wired, wireless and transmission media

Choose a medium by signal mechanism, distance, interference, capacity, mobility and installation.

Why might a hospital use fibre between buildings, copper to desk devices, WiFi for mobile equipment, microwaves to a nearby site and satellites for a remote clinic?

Before you start

Which two statements are wrong: fibre carries light, WiFi needs a cable to every device, microwave links need line of sight, or satellite communication has no time delay?

Check your answer

The WiFi and no-delay statements are wrong. WiFi uses radio waves, and a satellite signal takes time to travel through the uplink and downlink paths.

Wired and wireless create different constraints

S2.07.A01

Establish the concept

Wired devices send signals through a physical cable. A cable provides a guided path and can offer stable high capacity and reduced exposure to radio interference, but installation restricts movement and may require building work.

Wireless devices send electromagnetic waves through space. This supports mobility and flexible installation, but range, obstacles, competing signals and unauthorised reception attempts must be managed.

Security is not guaranteed by either category. Wired links still need access control and wireless links need strong authentication and encryption.

Different measurements answer different questions

Capacity / bandwidth in bit/sHow much data a link can carry per second under specified conditions.Enough usable capacity is needed for the workload.
Actual transfer rateData successfully delivered per second during this transfer.Sharing, protocol overhead and weaker signals can reduce it below a nominal capacity.
LatencyTime taken for data to travel or for a response to arrive; response time also includes processing.Interactive work can feel slow even on a link that carries large files quickly.
AttenuationSignal strength decreases during propagation.Longer distances may require suitable media or regeneration.
InterferenceUnwanted signals disturb reception.Electrical noise matters for copper and radio; fibre is not affected by electromagnetic interference in the same way.

Connect mobility and installation to the choice

A fixed desktop has a convenient cable endpoint; a walking tablet does not. A wireless link avoids a cable to the moving device, but shared coverage, obstruction and interference affect service. A cable requires installation and constrains movement, while offering a more controlled path. Compare a particular link and task, not a universal claim that every wired link is faster than every wireless link.

Core explanation · Exam essentials

  • Compare capacity, achieved rate and latency separately.
  • Distance, interference, mobility and installation constraints determine suitability.
Common misconception

Wireless does not mean unlimited range; WiFi coverage and capacity depend on the environment and access points.

Check your understanding

A link transfers a large file quickly but has long response delays. Which two properties should you distinguish?

Check your answer

Its achieved transfer rate can be high while its latency is also high; bit/s and time delay measure different properties.

02

Copper cable, fibre-optic cable and radio waves including WiFi

S2.08.A01S2.08.A02S2.08.A03

Visual overview

Establish the concept

Copper cable carries changing electrical signals. It is widely available and relatively inexpensive, but suffers attenuation and can be affected by electromagnetic interference; shielding and twisted pairs reduce interference.

Fibre-optic cable carries pulses of light through glass or plastic. It supports high data capacity, low attenuation over long distances and immunity to electromagnetic interference, but equipment and installation can cost more and the fibre needs careful handling.

Radio waves, including WiFi, carry data through space and provide mobility within a coverage area. They can pass through some obstacles, but distance, walls, competing transmitters and shared spectrum can weaken the signal or reduce capacity. Wireless access must use authentication and encryption because signals can extend beyond the intended room or building.

Follow the carrier and its practical consequence

MediumHow data is representedWhy select it / what limits it
Copper Ethernet cableChanging electrical signals travel through conductors.Economical for short fixed runs; attenuation and electromagnetic interference limit suitable runs and environments.
Fibre-optic cableLight pulses travel through optical fibre.High capacity over suitable long runs and immunity to electrical interference; installation, termination and equipment can cost more.
Radio / WiFi accessElectromagnetic waves travel through space to an access point or another radio receiver.Mobility and cable-free access; coverage, obstacles, interference and sharing affect achieved service.

Avoid overlapping-category mistakes

WiFi is a family of wireless networking technologies using radio waves; it is not a synonym for the internet. Microwaves are also part of the radio spectrum. The next unit uses “microwave link” for a directional point-to-point application, separating its installation requirements from the local WiFi access example. A signal carrier and the service carried over it are different classifications.

Core explanation · Exam essentials

  • Copper carries electrical signals; fibre carries light; wireless links use electromagnetic waves.
  • Match the medium to distance, noise, cost and movement; WiFi is local access rather than the internet itself.
Common misconception

Fibre carries light rather than electrical pulses in its core.

WiFi is one use of radio waves; it is not the name for every wireless transmission method.

Check your understanding

Why is fibre suitable for a long link across an electrically noisy factory?

Check your answer

It supports appropriate long high-capacity links and carries light rather than an electrical signal susceptible to the factory’s electromagnetic interference.

03

Microwaves

S2.08.A04

Visual overview

Establish the concept

Microwaves are high-frequency electromagnetic waves. A narrow, directional beam can carry data between fixed antennas at two sites, providing a high-bandwidth point-to-point link without installing cable between the sites.

The transmitting and receiving antennas must be accurately aligned and normally need a clear line of sight. Buildings, hills and the curvature of the Earth can block the signal, so tall masts or relay stations may be needed over a long distance.

Microwaves are useful between buildings or across ground where laying cable is difficult or expensive. Interference, movement of an antenna and bad weather can weaken the signal and make the link less reliable.

Evaluate a link between two fixed buildings

  1. Requirement: Buildings face each other across ground where digging is prohibited. Both have suitable mounting positions and a clear line of sight.
  2. Mechanism: Aligned directional antennas send and receive microwave signals along the unobstructed path. Pointing and clear propagation space matter.
  3. Choice: A terrestrial microwave link avoids laying cable across that ground and can carry the required traffic if the selected equipment and link conditions support it.
  4. Changed condition: A new obstruction blocks the path. Repositioning antennas, using a suitable relay or selecting another route/medium is needed; simply labelling the connection wireless does not remove the obstruction.

Core explanation · Exam essentials

  • Directional terrestrial microwave links need an appropriate clear, aligned path.
  • Justify them when cable installation is difficult and the path and capacity are suitable.
Common misconception

Microwaves are not the same as local WiFi and do not pass through every obstacle; fixed directional antennas normally need line of sight.

Check your understanding

Why would a newly built tower between two microwave antennas matter?

Check your answer

It can obstruct the propagation path and weaken or block reception, so the original line-of-sight assumption no longer holds.

04

Satellites

S2.08.A05

Visual overview

Establish the concept

A ground station sends microwaves to a satellite using an uplink. The satellite receives the signal and sends it back to one or more receiving dishes using a downlink.

Satellites can cover a very large geographical area and reach ships, aircraft and remote locations where installing cable is difficult or impossible. The same transmission can also be sent across a wide area.

Signals travel a very long distance to and from a satellite, so there is a time delay. This can affect telephone calls, video conferencing and real-time control. Bad weather can weaken the signal, and satellite equipment and services can be expensive.

Carry a message from an offshore vessel

  1. Uplink: The vessel’s terminal transmits a signal towards a suitable satellite. Antenna visibility, equipment and service coverage must support the connection.
  2. Relay and downlink: The satellite relays the communication to a ground station or another supported route; terrestrial provider infrastructure carries it onward to the destination service.
  3. Response: The service response travels back through the provider and satellite system to the vessel. Both outward and return paths contribute delay.
  4. Decision: Satellite can reach an offshore location lacking cable and terrestrial cellular coverage, with equipment, service cost, weather effects and propagation delay to consider.

Core explanation · Exam essentials

  • Satellite communication uses an uplink, relay and downlink to connect distant coverage areas.
  • Choose it for required coverage while considering service cost, visibility and delay.
Optional extension: orbit and propagation delay

A farther satellite generally creates a longer propagation path and more delay for comparable endpoints. Lower-orbit systems can reduce this part of delay but require changing satellite coverage and supporting infrastructure. Actual end-to-end delay also includes routing and processing; no single delay value applies to every satellite service.

Common misconception

Satellite communication is not local WiFi and it is not delay-free; every signal must travel through both an uplink and a downlink path.

Check your understanding

Does a high satellite download rate prove that an interactive call will have little delay?

Check your answer

No. Transfer capacity and propagation delay are separate; a long signal path can add delay even when the data rate is high.

Establish the concept

Choose a transmission medium by matching its physical carrier and operating characteristics to the scenario. Relevant factors include distance, required bandwidth, attenuation, interference, mobility, line of sight, time delay, installation constraints, security controls and cost.

A justified choice states the medium, names a specific characteristic and explains the consequence in the given setting. For example, fibre suits a long link near electrical machinery because light transmission is not affected by electromagnetic interference; satellites suit a remote vessel because their wide coverage does not require cable to be installed along the whole route.

Build a defensible selection

State the deciding condition, choose a suitable medium, then explain one relevant cost or limitation. If the conditions change, the conclusion may change. Do not claim fibre is always cheapest, satellite always has one fixed delay, or WiFi always supplies its advertised rate. Where several media meet the requirement, justify the selected trade-off rather than pretending only one name is possible.

Choose a medium

  1. NeedDistance, mobility, capacity and availability
  2. EnvironmentObstacles, interference, cable route and line of sight
  3. Trade-offInstallation, equipment, security and running cost
  4. JustifyLink the chosen carrier feature to the scenario consequence

Core explanation · Exam essentials

  • Use the scenario’s distance, noise, movement, installation and coverage requirements.
  • Explain one causal advantage and a relevant limitation, then reconsider changed conditions.
Common misconception

No medium is always best. A valid recommendation must connect a named characteristic to the actual distance, environment, mobility or delay requirement.

Check your understanding

If the fixed 30 m desktop becomes a tablet used while walking, why might the original copper choice change?

Check your answer

Mobility becomes the deciding condition; WiFi avoids a tethered device while requiring adequate coverage and shared capacity.

Question 1 · Command word: Compare6 teacher marks

Compare a wired connection with WiFi for tablets used around a warehouse in terms of mobility, transmission conditions and physical installation.

S2.07.A01S2.08.A03
Answer and checking points — teacher-written
  1. A wired tablet is constrained by its cable length and connection point.
  2. WiFi allows the tablet to move within the access point coverage area.
  3. A cable provides a guided signal path and avoids sharing the local radio spectrum.
  4. WiFi can suffer interference, obstacles and competition for radio capacity in the warehouse.
  5. Wired access requires a cable run and network port at each working position.
  6. WiFi reduces end-device cabling, although access points still need installation and connection to the LAN.

Common error: Use the same comparison factors for both options; a wired connection is not immune to all faults.

Question 2 · Command word: Explain4 teacher marks

Explain suitable transmission choices for (a) a 500 m link across an electrically noisy factory, (b) moving classroom tablets and (c) fixed buildings with a clear aligned path where cables cannot be installed. Give one limitation of the choice for the buildings.

S2.07.A01S2.08.A02S2.08.A03S2.08.A04S2.08.A06
Answer and checking points — teacher-written
  1. (a) Fibre is suitable for the distance and is not susceptible to electromagnetic interference in its light carrier.
  2. (b) WiFi gives moving tablets cable-free access within coverage.
  3. (c) A directional microwave link can connect the fixed buildings without installing cable across the gap.
  4. The microwave link needs a suitable unobstructed aligned path, so a new obstruction can impair it.

Common error: State the deciding property of each scenario, not a universal media speed ranking.

Question 1 · Command word: Describe10 teacher marks

Describe the characteristics of copper cable, fibre-optic cable, radio waves including WiFi, microwaves and satellites.

S2.08.A01S2.08.A02S2.08.A03S2.08.A04S2.08.A05
Answer and checking points — teacher-written
  1. Copper carries electrical signals through a guided cable path.
  2. Copper is relatively inexpensive but suffers attenuation and electromagnetic interference.
  3. Fibre carries pulses of light through glass or plastic.
  4. Fibre provides high bandwidth, low attenuation and immunity to electromagnetic interference, but installation and termination can cost more.
  5. WiFi uses radio waves through space and provides mobility within its coverage area.
  6. WiFi is affected by distance, obstacles, interference, shared spectrum and wireless-security requirements.
  7. Microwaves use an aligned directional beam between fixed antennas and can provide a high-bandwidth point-to-point link without cable between the sites.
  8. Microwaves normally need line of sight and can be weakened by obstruction, misalignment, interference or bad weather.
  9. Satellites use an uplink and downlink to provide very wide coverage, including remote or moving locations.
  10. Satellite communication can be affected by time delay, bad weather and higher equipment or service cost.

Common error: Do not describe every wireless medium as WiFi; distinguish local radio coverage, directional microwaves and a satellite uplink/downlink.

Question 2 · Command word: Suggest6 teacher marks

Suggest a medium for a high-capacity link between two school buildings and a different medium for laptops that move around classrooms. Justify each choice and give one drawback of each.

S2.07.A01S2.08.A02S2.08.A03S2.08.A06
Answer and checking points — teacher-written
  1. Fibre-optic cable is suitable between the buildings.
  2. Its high capacity and low attenuation support the inter-building link.
  3. Fibre installation and termination can be expensive.
  4. WiFi is suitable for the classroom laptops.
  5. Radio access allows the laptops to move within coverage without individual trailing cables.
  6. Obstacles, interference or shared radio capacity can reduce the wireless connection quality.

Common error: One medium need not solve every part of the scenario.
