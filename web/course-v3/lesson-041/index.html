Ethics and ownership · Concept 01

Recognise the intelligent task

Observe

Look, then discuss

How is recognising a photographed word different from saving text someone has already typed?

Compare a file containing the typed word “Exit” with a photograph of a handwritten version. The first already has characters supplied by a person. The second has pixel patterns that must be interpreted to produce characters.

Explain

Explanation · 1 / 4

Define AI through what it does

Artificial intelligence enables computers to perform tasks associated with human intelligence, such as recognising patterns, interpreting language, reasoning and predicting likely events. In the assistant, interpreting an image as words is the relevant task. Simply storing a typed string or playing an audio file does not by itself demonstrate AI.

Explanation · 2 / 4

Distinguish two broad approaches

A rule-based AI system applies expert-written knowledge to supplied facts to infer a conclusion. A data-driven approach learns patterns from examples. These are ways to explain where the system’s knowledge comes from; not every AI application learns from examples or changes its knowledge while being used.

Explanation · 3 / 4

Follow learning and checking

In a data-driven pump-monitoring example, past vibration and temperature readings are paired with known fault outcomes. A learning procedure adjusts a model to patterns in those examples. Separate known-outcome cases are then used to check its predictions. Checking only the examples used for learning would give insufficient evidence about new cases.

Explanation · 4 / 4

Follow inference on a new case

Inference means using the resulting model or knowledge to produce a conclusion from supplied input. A new pump reading can produce a predicted fault risk without updating the model. The future outcome is not known merely because past examples had known outcomes. A prediction can be wrong and should be interpreted with the relevant evidence.

Work together

A model trained last month reads a new page

The assistant’s recognition model was fitted using examples of text images and their known characters. Today it reads a new page without changing its parameters.

Work together · 1 / 3

Identify the earlier stage

Fitting the model from example images was learning. Testing on separate checked pages supplied evidence about recognition beyond the examples used for fitting.

Work together · 2 / 3

Identify today’s stage

Applying the existing model to the new photograph is inference. The output is a proposed character sequence, not a guarantee that every character matches the page.

Work together · 3 / 3

Explain a new difficulty

An unfamiliar font or a blurred image can differ from the conditions in the learning and test examples. Check performance in the intended classroom setting rather than assuming earlier success settles every new case.

Learning builds or adjusts a model; inference uses it. Neither requires a human-shaped robot or guarantees a correct result.

Check

Explain it yourself · Teacher-written

Check your reasoning

The second describes pattern recognition used to infer characters from an image. Storing already typed characters alone establishes storage, not an intelligent inference task. The recognition can still be wrong.

Check a common misunderstanding

AI is not limited to robots or systems that learn continuously, and an AI label does not prove accuracy.

Ethics and ownership · Concept 02

Follow a page from pixels to spoken words

Observe

Look, then discuss

Which transformations are needed before the student can hear a French sign in English?

A camera photographs a clear sign reading “Sortie”, and the user selects English output. Follow the information after each stage. Predict which stage first produces text and which later stage produces an audio waveform.

Recall

Keep representations distinct

Pixels describe the image. Recognised characters represent text. An audio waveform represents sound. A camera supplies an image; a speaker produces sound. A clear sound does not tell us whether the earlier text was correct.

Explain

Explanation · 1 / 5

Capture an image

The camera supplies pixel data representing the printed sign. At this point the program has an image of the word; it does not yet have a recognised character sequence. Lighting, focus and framing can affect the quality of the input.

Explanation · 2 / 5

Recognise characters with OCR

Optical character recognition, or OCR, locates character patterns and interprets them as text. Here it should produce the characters in “Sortie”. Recognising a character is different from translating its meaning into another language.

Explanation · 3 / 5

Interpret language and translate

Natural language processing uses information about words, phrases and context to interpret the recognised sequence. Translation produces corresponding text in the chosen language. In this sign context, the French text “Sortie” becomes the English text “Exit”. These are distinct conceptual tasks even if one product performs them together.

Explanation · 4 / 5

Generate and output speech

Text-to-speech generates an audio waveform representing the translated words. The speaker turns the output signal into sound, so the student hears “Exit”. Speech-to-text works in the reverse direction, recognising words from audio; it is not the final conversion used here.

Explanation · 5 / 5

Trace a fault to its stage

If a blurred letter becomes the wrong recognised word, later translation and clear speech can carry that error forward. Retaking the image or verifying the recognised text addresses the early problem. Increasing volume cannot repair a wrong word. If translated text is already correct but no audio is heard, investigate the later speech and output stages.

Work together

Trace the expected output and a changed condition

The clear sign reads “Sortie”. The target is English. In a second attempt, the photograph is too blurred for a checked recognition.

Work together · 1 / 3

State each intermediate representation

Camera: pixels showing the sign. OCR: text “Sortie”. Language processing and translation: interpreted French sign text and English text “Exit”. Text-to-speech: an audio waveform. Speaker: audible English speech.

Work together · 2 / 3

Locate the uncertainty in the second attempt

The blurred image makes character recognition uncertain before translation. Stop treating the output as a checked reading. Retake the photograph or compare the recognised text with a reliable reading of the sign.

Work together · 3 / 3

Explain the consequence

A polished voice can speak an incorrect translation derived from incorrect text. The quality of the final sound therefore cannot establish that all earlier transformations were accurate.

Describe input → transformation → intermediate output → use, and fix a fault at the stage where the evidence locates it.

Ethics and ownership · Concept 03

Transfer the input-to-use method

Observe

Look, then discuss

Does a model’s warning establish that a pump is faulty or a payment is fraudulent?

A pump produces a vibration reading that differs from its usual pattern. The model raises a fault-risk alert, and a technician decides whether to inspect it. Locate the model’s output and the separate action taken using that output.

Explain

Explanation · 1 / 4

Use a reusable explanation structure

Name the input representation, the task performed, the output and how someone uses it. “AI is used in healthcare” names a sector but does not explain an application. A precise description might explain that a model recognises suspicious patterns in a medical image and flags an area for a clinician to inspect.

Explanation · 2 / 4

Distinguish a prediction from an action

A maintenance model may estimate that a pump is likely to fail. The technician then inspects it and schedules work. The prediction is information supplied to a decision; the inspection is a subsequent action. Their separation matters when deciding who checks an uncertain result.

Explanation · 3 / 4

Transfer the structure to other settings

A fraud system can compare a new payment with transaction patterns and flag unusual activity for investigation. A recommendation system ranks items using preferences and item features. An autonomous vehicle interprets sensor data about roads and obstacles to support control decisions. The input and consequence differ, even though each uses recognition or inference.

ApplicationInput and AI taskOutput and use
Medical reviewMedical image; recognise suspicious patternsFlagged area for clinical review
Fraud detectionTransaction data; identify unusual patternsRisk flag for investigation
RecommendationsPreferences and item features; rank likely interestsOrdered suggestions for a user
Autonomous vehicleCamera and other sensor data; interpret obstaclesInformation used in vehicle control

Explanation · 4 / 4

Explain errors in the actual context

A false pump alert can cause an unnecessary inspection; a missed fault can allow a breakdown. A mistaken fraud flag can delay a legitimate payment. These are different consequences of particular outputs. State the error, affected person and result rather than saying only that AI sometimes fails.

Work together

A recycling sorter makes a wrong classification

A camera photographs an item on a sorting belt. A model classifies its material, and a controller directs it to a bin.

Work together · 1 / 2

Describe the recognition

The image is the input. The model interprets visual patterns to infer a material category. The resulting category is used by the controller to choose a bin.

Work together · 2 / 2

Explain the error’s consequence

If a non-recyclable item is classified as recyclable, the controller may place it into a recyclable-material stream and contaminate that stream. The physical movement alone does not establish AI; the image interpretation is the relevant task.

A useful application explanation names both the inference and the decision that uses it.

Check

Explain it yourself · Teacher-written

A fraud model flags a payment. Explain why “the customer committed fraud” is a stronger claim than the evidence supports.

Check your reasoning

The flag indicates a pattern associated with risk, not proof of wrongdoing. The payment needs investigation using relevant evidence. A false flag can affect a legitimate customer, so the response should account for that possibility.

Connect

Keep this idea

Describe the AI task precisely, then explain how its output is used and what an error would change.

The reading assistant’s value depends on which people it helps and whether its errors affect them equally.

Check a common misunderstanding

Naming an industry or a robot does not explain what the AI interprets, predicts or reasons about.

Ethics and ownership · Concept 04

Who gains access, and who still faces a barrier?

Observe

Look, then discuss

Can a 95% overall success rate hide a serious accessibility problem?

First identify two different barriers the reading assistant can address: difficulty seeing printed words and difficulty understanding their language. Then examine a related captioning example in which the same system produces different results for two accents.

Explain

Explanation · 1 / 6

Explain a social benefit through a person’s access

Social effects concern participation, access and treatment. A visually impaired reader can hear information they cannot easily see. A reader who does not understand the printed language can hear a translation in a language they understand. These are distinct benefits because they remove different barriers.

Explanation · 2 / 6

Read the counts behind the average

In this invented caption test, accent A has 90 correct captions out of 90 and accent B has 5 out of 10. Overall, 95 out of 100 are correct, or 95%. For B, however, only 50% are correct. The larger group dominates the overall result.

Explanation · 3 / 6

Explain the unequal effect without inventing a cause

Users relying on captions for group B receive less reliable access in this test. Different training examples, audio quality or operating conditions might contribute, but these counts alone do not prove the cause. Investigate representative cases and the conditions before claiming why the difference occurred.

Explanation · 4 / 6

Use workable correction and privacy controls

A correction route helps only if someone can identify the error and supply accurate text in time. Human reviewers need relevant skills, evidence and authority to act. Privacy is a separate concern: recordings and photographed pages can reveal personal information, so limit unnecessary collection and retention and restrict access.

Explanation · 5 / 6

Relate the test to the proposed use

A short practice caption and an emergency instruction have different consequences if wrong. Compare performance and correction arrangements with the actual users and task. A high average alone cannot justify removing an existing accessible, checked resource.

Explanation · 6 / 6

Transfer the reasoning to classroom monitoring

A camera-based system might analyse classroom behaviour to identify students who need extra support. This could improve access to help. Constant observation or sharing recorded behaviour may also intrude on privacy and cause stress. Explain the affected people and the mechanism of the effect; calling the system simply “good” or “unethical” leaves the impact unexplained.

Work together

Explain two benefits and one condition

The school’s assistant photographs a notice in French, translates it into English and reads it aloud.

Work together · 1 / 3

Explain the sight-related benefit

A visually impaired student can hear the notice instead of depending on being able to read its small print. The spoken result provides another route to the information.

Work together · 2 / 3

Explain the language-related benefit

A student who understands English but not French can receive the meaning in a language they know. This is a different barrier from the ability to see the print.

Work together · 3 / 3

State the condition for access

Both benefits depend on sufficiently accurate reading and translation and a usable correction alternative. Incorrect output can leave the student with confidently delivered but misleading information.

Explain who is helped, what barrier is reduced and what conditions affect that benefit.

Check your reasoning

Inspect the tested and correct counts for relevant groups, the conditions and the representativeness of the samples. Compare access and correction options. The total alone can hide unequal errors and cannot establish their cause or future performance.

Cambridge 9618/12 · May/June 2025 · 3(a), 3(b) · 6 marks

Follow the product’s sequence: camera image, recognised words, translation, spoken output. For social benefits, identify people helped and what they can do more easily.

Reasoning and model answer — teacher-written

3(a): Optical character recognition locates characters in the camera image and converts their pixel patterns into text. Natural language processing combines the recognised words into meaningful phrases and interprets their context. A translation system produces the corresponding words in the chosen language. Text-to-speech turns the translated text into an audio waveform that the user hears.

“AI reads it” names no process. Recognising the characters and translating their meaning are different stages. Speed alone needs a clear social benefit in this situation.

Ethics and ownership · Concept 05

Compare useful work with the complete cost

Observe

Look, then discuss

If the assistant saves preparation time, does it necessarily save money?

The school expects fewer hours of routine transcription. It also needs a service subscription, someone to check output, repairs to incorrect material and an initial setup. Separate recurring changes from the one-off setup before comparing totals.

Explain

Explanation · 1 / 4

Explain the productivity mechanism

Productivity concerns output from resources such as staff time. Automated transcription may let a teacher prepare more accessible material in the same time. In a factory, a useful prediction may avoid an unplanned shutdown. Explain the task that changes and the resulting saving instead of claiming that all AI reduces costs.

Explanation · 2 / 4

Include the added work and expenditure

Costs can include purchase or development, integration, data preparation, subscriptions, computing, maintenance, staff training and review. Incorrect output can cause rework or other losses. The same activity and time period must be compared with the previous method.

Explanation · 3 / 4

Work through a stated monthly example

These are invented teaching values in currency units. The previous method costs 1200 per month for the same work. The new service costs 500, review costs 400, maintenance costs 100 and rework costs 100. The recurring saving is 1200 − 500 − 400 − 100 − 100 = 100. If setup costs another 600, the first-month change is 100 − 600 = −500: a net additional cost of 500.

Explanation · 4 / 4

Distinguish saved time from cash and employment

Putting a value on released staff time does not necessarily reduce the salary bill. Staff may use that time for other work. Automation can reduce some tasks while creating demand for oversight, maintenance or specialist support. Some people may need retraining or lose work, even when the organisation as a whole becomes more productive.

Work together

Change the review and correction workload

Use the monthly teaching example: the previous method costs 1200, while the AI-assisted method has service 500, review 400, maintenance 100 and rework 100. Setup is a separate one-off cost of 600.

Work together · 1 / 3

Establish the recurring comparison

The recurring total is 500 + 400 + 100 + 100 = 1100. Compared with 1200 for the previous method, the recurring net saving is 100 under these assumptions. Over six months, 600 of operating savings offsets the 600 setup cost; other unchanged costs are excluded equally.

Work together · 2 / 3

Change the workload

If review rises to 700 and rework rises to 250 each month, the recurring total becomes 500 + 700 + 100 + 250 = 1550. That is 350 more per month than the previous method, before setup. Increased checking and correction can reverse the economic conclusion.

Work together · 3 / 3

Limit a real-world financial claim

The example treats the figures as costs. In a real proposal, establish whether claimed avoided work actually reduces expenditure or merely releases staff time for other useful tasks. Include setup when evaluating the initial period.

A credible economic comparison includes recurring costs, initial costs, errors and what actually happens to released staff time.

Ethics and ownership · Concept 06

Compare resource savings with the system’s footprint

Observe

Look, then discuss

Does an AI controller save electricity if its own operation uses more than it avoids?

Use an independent irrigation example. The old method uses 1000 kWh per month for pumping. An AI controller reduces pumping to 850 kWh, but computing and cooling add electricity use. Compare the complete operating totals before describing the change.

Explain

Explanation · 1 / 5

Identify possible resource savings

AI-assisted irrigation can avoid unnecessary watering and pumping. Route planning may reduce vehicle travel, and predictive maintenance may extend equipment life. Each claimed environmental benefit needs a mechanism and a comparison with the previous or simpler method serving the same need.

Explanation · 2 / 5

Include the system’s own requirements

Learning and inference use computing equipment and electricity. Cooling can use energy and water. Manufacturing, replacing and disposing of sensors and computing hardware use materials and create environmental impacts. The amount depends on the particular system and its lifetime.

Explanation · 3 / 5

Add operating values with the same units

Under comparable monthly watering conditions, the baseline is 1000 kWh. With pumping at 850 kWh and added AI plus cooling at 80 kWh, the new total is 930 kWh and the operating saving is 70 kWh. If the added use rises to 180 kWh, the total is 1030 kWh: 30 kWh more than the baseline.

Baseline1000 kWh0 kWh additional1000 kWh
Case A850 kWh80 kWh930 kWh
Case B850 kWh180 kWh1030 kWh

Explanation · 4 / 5

Limit the conclusion to the evidence

These invented values establish operating-electricity differences within the stated boundary. They do not measure manufacturing impacts, water use, disposal or emissions per kWh. Do not subtract litres of water from kWh of electricity. A broader environmental judgement needs evidence about each relevant measure and the system lifetime.

Explanation · 5 / 5

Extend the boundary using an explicit assumption

The activity below compares 12 months and adds an illustrative 1200 kWh for making the new hardware, allocated evenly across its assumed lifetime. A 60-month lifetime allocates 1200 × 12 / 60 = 240 kWh to the comparison. For case A, 12 × 930 + 240 = 11400 kWh, compared with the baseline 12000. If the lifetime is only 12 months, all 1200 kWh is allocated and the total becomes 12360. This is an accounting assumption for comparison; manufacturing electricity is used when the hardware is made.

Work together

Reconsider a claim of environmental improvement

A supplier advertises “150 kWh saved” because pumping falls from 1000 to 850 kWh. Additional computing and cooling use is 180 kWh per month.

Work together · 1 / 3

Locate the incomplete boundary

The claim counts only pumping. The added system consumes 180 kWh, so the combined operating total is 850 + 180 = 1030 kWh.

Work together · 2 / 3

State the supported conclusion

Operating electricity increases by 30 kWh compared with the stated baseline. Other benefits, such as water savings, might still exist, but these values do not quantify them or establish an overall environmental improvement.

Work together · 3 / 3

Identify evidence still needed

Compare water use, equipment manufacture and replacement, disposal and the intended operating lifetime. Use comparable service conditions so that an apparent saving is not merely less irrigation being delivered.

A saving in one component can coexist with an increase in the complete operating total.

Ethics and ownership · Concept 07

Decide how the school should use the assistant

Observe

Look, then discuss

What evidence would justify replacing the school’s existing checked reading materials?

The assistant can produce material quickly, but some photographed pages are still misread. The school has checked transcripts, teachers with limited review time and a proposal to retain images for improvement. Decide what a responsible pilot must demonstrate before replacing the existing materials.

Explain

Explanation · 1 / 7

Define the actual decision

Adding an optional checked reading aid is different from replacing the only accessible resource. Identify the input, AI task, output and intended use, then state what changes for the users. The consequences of an error depend on that use.

Explanation · 2 / 7

Connect evidence across the chapter

Social evidence concerns access, unequal errors and privacy. Economic evidence concerns useful time released, integration, operation, review and correction costs. Environmental evidence concerns the additional equipment and resource use compared with the previous method. Include relevant effects rather than inserting an unrelated stock advantage from another application.

Explanation · 3 / 7

Check whether the controls can work

A reviewer needs access to a reliable original, sufficient language knowledge, time and authority to correct the output. An inaccessible complaints form is not an effective correction route. Check samples beyond the cases already flagged as uncertain, because confidently wrong outputs may otherwise be missed.

Explanation · 4 / 7

Compare alternatives and set a reviewable decision

The school could retain checked transcripts while running a limited assisted pilot. It should define which uses are permitted, who checks output, what information is retained and how errors are reported. Evidence from representative users, correction capacity and practical costs can then support expansion, revision or stopping the pilot.

Explanation · 5 / 7

State what would change the conclusion

A recommendation should identify remaining uncertainty and the evidence that would resolve it. If the assistant works well for all intended groups and corrections are timely within available resources, a wider controlled deployment may become justified. If it repeatedly leaves some students without reliable access, the school should change the arrangement rather than rely on an average score.

Explanation · 6 / 7

Transfer responsibilities to the school network

Students using connected tools also make decisions that affect others. Respect other people’s privacy, avoid bullying through the network and acknowledge work created by someone else. Do not present another person’s work as your own. Each action connects to a person’s information, wellbeing or contribution, rather than simply repeating that the network should be used responsibly.

Explanation · 7 / 7

Evaluate AI homework help through changed learning activity

An AI tutor can offer additional explanations and practice at a student’s own pace, which may build confidence. Reliance on supplied answers can reduce the student’s own reasoning practice, while replacing discussion with individual screen use may reduce collaboration and social contact. Access can also be unequal if some students lack suitable equipment. These are possible effects of how the tool is used, not inevitable outcomes for every student.

Work together

Should automatic captions replace checked transcripts?

Use the invented caption evidence: group A has 90/90 correct results and group B has 5/10. Checked transcripts exist. The proposed reviewer cannot reliably understand group B’s speech.

Work together · 1 / 4

Identify the benefit and the specific risk

Captions could appear faster, but B currently receives a correct caption in only half of its tested cases. Removing checked transcripts would remove a working alternative for those users.

Work together · 2 / 4

Test the proposed safeguard

The named reviewer cannot reliably compare B’s speech with the generated text. Their presence therefore does not establish an effective correction process for the observed problem.

Work together · 3 / 4

Reach a supported conclusion

Retain checked transcripts and use a limited assisted pilot while arranging competent correction and representative testing. Do not replace the checked material solely because the overall result is 95%.

Work together · 4 / 4

Explain when to reconsider

Reassess once group-level performance, correction turnaround and reviewer capacity meet the school’s agreed access requirements, with acceptable costs and information-handling arrangements. Improved evidence can justify a revised decision.

A supported evaluation chooses an action, links it to the evidence and states workable conditions and remaining limits.

1. Apply ethical responsibilities and assess AI homework impacts

Cambridge 9618/11 · October/November 2025 · 8(b), 8(c) · 7 marks

Selected parts: 8(b) [3]; 8(c) [4].

Hint — reading the question

Read the opening sentence before answering 8(b): spreading malware is already supplied, so give three other considerations. In 8(c), describe two social impacts on students and develop each with consequences in this homework context.

Reasoning and model answer — teacher-written
PartModel response
8(b): three other considerationsRespect other people’s privacy; do not use the network for cyberbullying; do not copy and present work that is not your own.
8(c): learning supportAdditional AI support may help students do better. Students who struggle in traditional lessons may also gain confidence by learning at their own pace.
8(c): communication and wellbeingHeavy reliance on AI may reduce collaboration and face-to-face communication. Increased screen time and isolation can also contribute to anxiety or loneliness.
Official mark scheme
Why this earns marks — teacher explanation

Part 8(b) awards one mark per accepted point, up to three. The scheme also accepts following school network rules, using the network for legitimate purposes and not hacking other computers. Repeating the supplied malware risk does not answer the request for other considerations.

Part 8(c) awards one mark per accepted bullet point, up to four. The model develops learning support with confidence and social participation with wellbeing, using points accepted in the scheme. Other accepted effects include reduced reasoning practice, unequal access to the technology, incorrect AI answers and changes to school assessment. The question does not demand one positive and one negative impact.

Common mistakes — teacher analysis

A list of four undeveloped labels does not meet the request to describe two impacts. Keep the response about students and learning; a claim that all AI answers are wrong or that every student becomes isolated is unsupported.

Question 1 · Command word: Explain2 teacher marks

Explain what is meant by artificial intelligence and distinguish recognising a handwritten postcode from storing a postcode typed by a user.

S7.06.A01
Answer and checking points — teacher-written
  1. AI performs tasks associated with human intelligence, such as interpreting patterns or making inferences.
  2. Handwriting recognition interprets an image to infer characters, while storing an already typed postcode alone does not demonstrate that task.

Common error: Do not define AI as all uses of computers or only as robots.

Question 2 · Command word: Describe3 teacher marks

An AI payment service flags unusual transactions for staff review. Describe what the model analyses and how its output is used; explain why a flag is not proof of fraud.

S7.06.A02
Answer and checking points — teacher-written
  1. The model examines features of a new payment in relation to transaction patterns.
  2. A risk flag directs staff attention to a payment needing investigation.
  3. A legitimate unusual payment can also be flagged, so further evidence is needed.

Common error: A prediction or classification may be wrong.

Question 3 · Command word: Explain3 teacher marks

Automatic captions work well for one accent but often misrecognise another. Explain one social benefit and one social risk of using them in an online lesson, and suggest a safeguard.

S7.06.A03
Answer and checking points — teacher-written
  1. Captions can give students who cannot hear the audio access to the spoken lesson.
  2. Students relying on poorly recognised speech may receive inaccurate information and unequal access.
  3. Provide a way to correct captions or supply checked lesson text for affected users.

Common error: Name the students affected and connect the safeguard to caption errors.

Question 4 · Command word: Explain3 teacher marks

A factory uses an AI system that forecasts machine faults. Explain one economic benefit, one system cost and one possible effect on workers.

S7.06.A04
Answer and checking points — teacher-written
  1. Earlier repair scheduling can reduce production time lost to unexpected breakdowns.
  2. Sensors, model operation and integration require expenditure even when forecasts are useful.
  3. Maintenance staff may need retraining to interpret alerts and plan work differently.

Common error: Do not treat a reduction in emergency repairs as elimination of all maintenance jobs.

Question 5 · Command word: Evaluate4 teacher marks

A recruitment firm wants AI to reject applicants automatically. Testing shows good overall accuracy but much poorer results for one group. Evaluate automatic rejection and recommend a safeguard.

S7.06.A03S7.06.A06
Answer and checking points — teacher-written
  1. Automated screening can process applications quickly, reducing routine staff workload.
  2. The poorer group performance can unfairly exclude suitable applicants, so the overall average is insufficient evidence of fairness.
  3. Use audited assistance with human review and an appeal route rather than final rejection solely from the model.
  4. Deployment should depend on investigating the unequal errors and showing that review can address them.

Common error: A judgement must use the supplied group-performance evidence.

Question 6 · Command word: Calculate3 teacher marks

A delivery company compares two routing systems over the same month, with the same deliveries. The previous routes use 6000 kWh-equivalent of fuel energy. New routes use 5700, while the AI service adds 200 kWh of operating electricity, including cooling. Calculate the change in the stated operating-energy total. Then calculate it if the service instead adds 400 kWh, and explain why neither calculation alone proves an overall environmental improvement.

S7.06.A05S7.06.A06
Answer and checking points — teacher-written
  1. With 200 kWh additional operation, the new total is 5700 + 200 = 5900 kWh-equivalent, 100 less than the supplied 6000 baseline.
  2. With 400 kWh additional operation, the new total is 6100 kWh-equivalent, 100 more than the baseline despite shorter-route fuel savings.
  3. This is the supplied final-energy accounting comparison only. Fuel and electricity can have different emissions per unit; hardware manufacture, lifetime and disposal and other resource impacts are also uncounted, so a broader environmental claim needs further evidence.

Common error: Do not count cooling twice or treat equal energy quantities from different sources as necessarily equal emissions.

Question 7 · Command word: Describe5 teacher marks

A museum device photographs a printed Italian description, translates it into English and reads it aloud. Describe the role and intermediate output of OCR, language processing, translation and text-to-speech. A blurred image causes a wrong recognised word that is then spoken clearly: explain one suitable correction and why clear speech does not establish a correct reading.

S7.06.A02
Answer and checking points — teacher-written
  1. OCR locates character patterns in the camera image and converts them to text; pixels are not already a recognised character sequence.
  2. Language processing uses the recognised words and language/context to interpret the description; it may help resolve ambiguity but cannot guarantee repair of every wrong recognition.
  3. Translation produces English text from the interpreted source-language text.
  4. Text-to-speech generates the corresponding audio waveform for playback through the speaker.
  5. Retake the blurred image or verify and correct the recognised text before relying on the translation. Clear speech can reproduce an incorrect intermediate word, so it does not prove correct recognition.

Common error: The camera supplies images; speech-to-text is not the conversion from translated text to audio.

Question 8 · Command word: Describe3 teacher marks

Describe an AI speech-to-text application using its input, recognition task, output and practical use.

S7.06.A02
Answer and checking points — teacher-written
  1. Recorded or live speech supplies audio input.
  2. The system recognises speech patterns and infers the spoken words.
  3. It produces text that can be used for captions or dictation.

Common error: Connect the stages instead of listing unrelated AI application areas.

Question 9 · Command word: Explain3 teacher marks

An AI system reduces a building's heating demand but requires new servers. Explain one environmental benefit and two environmental costs that should be considered.

S7.06.A05
Answer and checking points — teacher-written
  1. Lower heating demand can reduce fuel or electricity consumption in the building.
  2. Running and cooling the servers consumes energy and may add emissions.
  3. Making and eventually replacing the servers uses materials and creates disposal impacts.

Common error: Include the AI equipment's footprint as well as the controlled building's saving.

Question 10 · Command word: Calculate4 teacher marks

In an invented caption test, group A has 160 correct captions out of 200 and group B has 20 correct captions out of 50. Each caption is checked against a reference and counted as a whole correct or incorrect item. Calculate the overall correct percentage and each group’s percentage, then explain one limitation of using the overall figure to approve deployment.

S7.06.A03S7.06.A06
Answer and checking points — teacher-written
  1. The overall rate is (160 + 20) / (200 + 50) × 100 = 72%.
  2. Group A has 160 / 200 × 100 = 80% correct.
  3. Group B has 20 / 50 × 100 = 40% correct.
  4. The overall rate weights the larger group more heavily and hides B’s much poorer access. Check representative group performance and workable correction before deployment; the discrepancy alone does not prove its cause.

Common error: Do not average 80% and 40% without weighting by the different numbers of tested captions.

Topic recap
  • Recognise the intelligent task

    Identify the recognition, reasoning or prediction task, and distinguish learning from later inference.

  • Follow a page from pixels to spoken words

    Distinguish OCR, language processing, translation and text-to-speech by the transformation each performs.

  • Transfer the input-to-use method

    Describe the AI task precisely, then explain how its output is used and what an error would change.

  • Who gains access, and who still faces a barrier?

    Connect social effects to access, privacy and treatment of named people; inspect differences hidden by an average.

  • Compare useful work with the complete cost

    Explain productivity through the changed task, then compare all relevant costs and effects on work.

  • Compare resource savings with the system’s footprint

    Compare the application and the added system against a consistent baseline; state the measure, period and limits.

  • Decide how the school should use the assistant

    Evaluate a particular use with evidence, alternatives and practical safeguards, then state a justified action.
