Ethical decisions and their consequences

Identify stakeholders, compare the consequences of two courses of action and justify a response to a specific ethical problem.

How can an ethical judgement show exactly who is affected and why?

Identify the decision before listing people

Start with the proposed action: what will someone collect, publish, change, certify or conceal? Then identify people connected to its consequences. Users and customers may be directly affected; colleagues, family members and the wider public can also be affected even if they never operate the system. Name the connection instead of writing only “society”.

Include the team doing the work

Towards colleagues, fair treatment avoids excluding people for irrelevant personal characteristics. Acknowledging contributions gives proper credit and supports trust within the team. Offering and accepting specific, constructive criticism allows technical work to improve; helping a colleague develop skills reduces dependence on unsupported guesses. These are professional responsibilities as well as interpersonal behaviour.

Relate different duties to their recipients

Towards the public, safeguard health and wellbeing, protect personal information and make realistic claims about software. Towards a client or employer, give competent advice, disclose relevant limitations and handle confidential information appropriately. A duty to meet a deadline does not justify misleading the client or harming the public.

Build a reason, not a label

Suppose a colleague finds an accessibility defect. Thank and credit the person who found it, review the evidence fairly and give specific feedback on the repair. Users need a truthful description of the route; the operator needs a realistic release recommendation. Each group is connected to the same incident by a different responsibility.

Core explanation · Exam essentials

  • Identify the decision, the affected people and a specific responsibility towards each.
  • Duties to colleagues include fairness, constructive criticism, recognising contributions and helping them develop.
Common misconception

A possible benefit does not automatically cause the possible harm; analyse each effect separately.

Check your understanding

A colleague’s test reveals a fault in your code. Give one action towards the colleague and one towards the public, explaining each.

Check your answer

Acknowledge the colleague’s contribution and discuss the evidence constructively so the work can improve. Correct or withdraw the misleading public claim so users do not rely on a known unsafe or unsuitable result.

02

Compare ethical and unethical actions

S7.03.A02

Visual overview

Follow the effect of each action

A consequence needs a causal link. Reporting a faulty route enables correction or a warning, which helps a traveller avoid an inaccessible journey. “Good reputation” skips the action and mechanism; explain that honest reporting gives people a better basis for trusting future information. A possible benefit on one branch is not the cause of a possible harm on the other.

Make a fair comparison

Keep the starting conditions fixed when comparing choices. If a route is already known to contain steps, compare reporting that fact with hiding that same fact. Do not silently assume that the fault vanishes in one branch or that every affected person necessarily suffers the worst possible outcome. Use “may” where the outcome is uncertain.

One fault, two actions and two stakeholder groups

Separate short-term cost from justification

Immediate expenditure is compatible with an ethical decision: testing and correction take time. Conversely, an apparent short-term saving from concealment can move costs and risks onto users. A good comparison recognises the practical cost of the responsible action while explaining why that cost does not justify hiding the defect.

Worked example · A travel application's accessibility error

  1. Situation

    A developer finds that a route labelled wheelchair-accessible includes steps. The manager wants to publish the route unchanged to meet a launch date.

  2. Ethical course

    Correct or withdraw the label and tell the travel operator about the uncertainty. Launch may be delayed, but wheelchair users can avoid an unsuitable route.

  3. Unethical course

    Keep the label and hide the report. A user may reach an inaccessible location and need assistance; the operator may receive complaints and lose trust.

  4. Comparison

    The deadline benefit does not remove the duty to provide reliable accessibility information. The correction addresses a foreseeable consequence for a specific group.

Core explanation · Exam essentials

  • Trace each proposed action to a consequence for a named stakeholder.
  • An ethical response can have immediate costs; compare supported consequences without treating every possible outcome as certain.
Common misconception

Simply writing 'good reputation' or 'bad reputation' leaves out the action and why it changes trust.

Check your understanding

Can an ethical response still cost the organisation money?

Check your answer

Yes. Correcting a problem may cost money or delay a release while preventing harm and supporting justified trust.

03

Justify a proportionate response

S7.03.A03

Visual overview

Build a supported judgement

  1. DecisionIdentify the proposed action and legitimate purpose.
  2. EvidenceConnect benefits, harms and duties to affected people.
  3. AlternativeTest whether a less intrusive option meets the purpose.
  4. ConclusionChoose an action with relevant conditions and remaining limits.

Compare a workable alternative

An evaluation is more than listing benefits and harms. State the legitimate purpose and ask whether the proposed action is needed to achieve it. A less intrusive or less harmful alternative is useful only if it actually meets that purpose. Proportionality means matching the action to the need rather than gathering as much information as technically possible.

Explain how the safeguard helps

Safeguards need mechanisms. Restricting access reduces the number of people able to view a record; a retention limit reduces how much old information remains available for misuse; an error-correction route helps people challenge an inaccurate record. None guarantees that all misuse or error will disappear.

Finish with a supported action

Separate a fact from an assumption. If the scenario does not tell you whether a safer alternative works, state the evidence needed or make the recommendation conditional. A conclusion should identify who will do what, why it is justified and what remaining limitation needs attention.

Worked example · Location collection: change the purpose, reconsider the decision

  1. Ordinary attendance

    A college proposes tracking students’ phones all day and night solely to record arrival at classes. A classroom check-in can meet that stated purpose.

  2. Choice and controls

    Use classroom check-in; restrict access to attendance staff and keep records only for a justified attendance period. Provide a correction route for failed check-ins.

  3. Reason and remaining limit

    Continuous off-site location history is unnecessary for confirming classroom arrival. Check-in errors remain possible, so a failed scan is not final proof of absence.

  4. Changed conditions

    During an organised outdoor trip, a student becomes separated from the group. The immediate purpose is locating that student safely; a classroom check-in cannot meet it.

  5. Reconsidered action

    Use a suitable available location service within the trip’s authorised safety arrangements, limiting access to the search team and collection to the emergency need. Verify the location because it may be stale or unavailable.

  6. After the event

    End emergency tracking and retain or remove records according to the justified follow-up need. The emergency does not establish a reason for permanent monitoring of everyone.

Core explanation · Exam essentials

  • Justify an action from the purpose, evidence, stakeholder effects and realistic alternatives.
  • Match each safeguard to a risk; change the recommendation when relevant conditions change.
Common misconception

A balanced response need not treat all choices as equally acceptable or end with an unsupported compromise.

Check your understanding

Does rejecting continuous tracking for attendance prove that location data must never be used?

Check your answer

No. Reassess a different purpose such as locating a missing person, considering necessity, alternatives, accuracy, authorised access and duration. A justified emergency use does not justify unrestricted ongoing tracking.

Practice questions

5 core tasks · 14 teacher marks · 24–32 minutes (teacher estimate). Explain your reasoning before checking the answer.

Question 1 · Command word: Identify2 teacher marks

A town publishes named residents' complaints on an open website to show how quickly repairs are handled. Identify two stakeholders and explain one concern for each.

S7.03.A01
Answer and checking points — teacher-written
  1. Residents who complained may have private contact or complaint details exposed.
  2. Town staff need to report performance without losing residents' trust through unnecessary disclosure.

Common error: Explain how each group is affected rather than listing unrelated organisations.

Question 2 · Command word: Explain2 teacher marks

A developer knows that a delivery app sends drivers to a closed bridge. Explain one consequence for drivers of reporting the error and one of concealing it.

S7.03.A02
Answer and checking points — teacher-written
  1. Reporting the defect allows the route to be corrected or warned against, helping drivers avoid wasted or unsafe journeys.
  2. Concealing it leaves drivers relying on a route that cannot be completed, causing delay and possible safety risks.

Common error: Use both actions and make the consequence specific to drivers.

Question 3 · Command word: Explain3 teacher marks

A response to an attendance proposal says, 'Tracking everyone is good because it is efficient.' Explain two weaknesses in this reasoning and suggest an alternative to continuous location tracking.

S7.03.A01S7.03.A03
Answer and checking points — teacher-written
  1. The claim does not identify whose efficiency improves or how an attendance action causes that improvement.
  2. Continuous location collection affects students’ privacy even outside the attendance decision, and its necessity has not been established.
  3. Record arrival at the classroom instead: this still establishes attendance while collecting less information about students’ movements.

Common error: Critique the supplied reasoning rather than inventing a different response.

Question 4 · Command word: Explain3 teacher marks

A programmer’s colleague finds an error in the programmer’s code for a public timetable. The programmer plans to dismiss the criticism and claim sole credit for the repair. Explain two responsible actions towards the colleague and one towards the public, giving a reason for each.

S7.03.A01S7.03.A02
Answer and checking points — teacher-written
  1. Discuss and accept evidence-based criticism constructively so that the error and the technical work can be improved.
  2. Acknowledge the colleague’s contribution to finding or repairing the error so that credit is fair and the colleague’s work is recognised.
  3. Correct the timetable or disclose its known limitation so members of the public do not unknowingly rely on incorrect journey information.

Common error: Address both recipients. Praise without a relevant action does not repair the professional problem.

Question 5 · Command word: Justify4 teacher marks

A library wants to publish borrowers’ names and loan histories to recover overdue books. Private reminders reach those borrowers. Later, a named borrower privately authorises a librarian to share the title of one lost book with a replacement supplier. Justify a response to each proposal and state one safeguard and one remaining risk for the supplier disclosure.

S7.03.A01S7.03.A03
Answer and checking points — teacher-written
  1. Use private reminders for overdue loans: they meet the stated purpose without exposing every borrower’s reading history.
  2. The later limited disclosure has a different stated purpose and the borrower’s authorisation; share only what the supplier needs for that replacement rather than treating it as permission to publish all loans.
  3. Verify the intended supplier/recipient and limit the information to the required book details, excluding unrelated borrowing history.
  4. A recipient could still retain or misuse the details; communicate the limited purpose and review what information genuinely needs to be sent. The control reduces rather than eliminates risk.

Common error: Neither “never share anything” nor “consent allows every future use” evaluates both sets of conditions.

Optional consolidation · 2 tasks · 5 teacher marks

Choose a task when you need another check of this idea.

Question 1 · Command word: Explain2 teacher marks

A company postpones a booking-system launch to correct a fault that loses reservations. Explain one short-term cost and one longer-term benefit of this decision.

S7.03.A02
Answer and checking points — teacher-written
  1. Postponement may delay booking revenue or require extra development expenditure.
  2. Fixing the fault reduces lost reservations and helps retain customers' confidence in future bookings.

Common error: Ethical behaviour does not imply an absence of immediate costs.

Question 2 · Command word: Justify3 teacher marks

A sports club needs emergency contacts for one weekend trip but proposes keeping them indefinitely for advertising. Justify a more appropriate use of the data and suggest two safeguards.

S7.03.A03
Answer and checking points — teacher-written
  1. Limit use to the trip's emergency purpose because advertising is a separate use the stated need does not justify.
  2. Restrict access to staff responsible for emergencies.
  3. Delete the list after the trip and any justified follow-up period instead of retaining it indefinitely.

Common error: Each safeguard must address access, purpose or retention in this scenario.

Original question · Cambridge 9618/12

1. Apply ethical responsibilities to two groups

Cambridge 9618/12 · October/November 2024 · 5(a) · 4 marks

Selected parts: 5(a) [4].

Reading the question — teacher guidance

Address colleagues and the public separately. A long answer about only one group is capped at two marks.

Solution / model answer — teacher-written

Towards colleagues, the programmer should give constructive criticism so that their colleagues can improve their work. Towards the public, the programmer should be honest and realistic about what the software can do so that the public can trust the claims made about it.

Official mark scheme
Why this earns marks — teacher explanation

The scheme allows at most two marks for colleagues and two for the public. Each sentence gives an action and its consequence. Other accepted actions include fair treatment, giving credit and protecting public health or personal information.

Common mistakes — teacher analysis

A general statement such as “be ethical to everyone” does not show how the responsibility applies to either group.
