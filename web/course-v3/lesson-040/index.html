Copyright and software licences

Explain copyright protection, distinguish software licensing approaches and choose a licence from stated permissions and requirements.

Which rights does a user need, and which licence actually grants them?

Before you start

A program is available to download without payment. Does that establish permission to change and redistribute its source?

Check your answer

No. Price does not establish those permissions; inspect the licence terms and source availability.

01

Why software needs copyright protection

S7.04.A01

Visual overview

Explain the need for protection

Creating software can take substantial skill, time and investment, while reproducing the finished work is inexpensive. Without enforceable rights, others could distribute the work without respecting the creator’s terms. Copyright provides a basis for challenging unauthorised copying and for granting permissions that can support continued development.

Identify what is protected

The protected subject includes original program expression and documentation. Copyright does not give ownership of every underlying idea, procedure or mathematical method. Writing an independent implementation of a general idea and copying protected source code are different acts; the exact legal position also depends on applicable law and exceptions.

Separate ownership from use

A licence is permission to perform particular acts under stated conditions. Buying a copy normally obtains the permitted use, not ownership of the copyright. The rights holder can authorise redistribution while retaining copyright and requiring notices to remain. An open licence therefore uses copyright permissions rather than cancelling ownership.

Avoid a registration misconception

Copyright generally arises automatically for qualifying software; a notice or record of authorship can help identify the rights holder. Do not teach that paying a fee, adding a symbol or registering every program is universally required before any protection exists. The practical task here is to identify the rights and the permissions needed, rather than memorise a country’s legal procedures.

Worked example · A purchased program copied to a class

  1. Situation

    A tutor buys software whose licence permits installation on one machine. The tutor wants to copy it to twenty classroom computers.

  2. Read the permission

    The payment obtained a one-machine permission; it did not transfer the source code's copyright or grant twenty installations.

  3. Decision

    Obtain suitable classroom permissions or select another tool whose licence permits the intended installations.

  4. Reason

    Cheap copying does not remove the creator's enforceable rights or enlarge the licence that was purchased.

Core explanation · Exam essentials

  • Copyright protects software expression and supports control of restricted copying and distribution.
  • A licence grants specified permissions; purchasing a copy or publishing source does not itself transfer copyright.
Common misconception

Permission to download one copy is not unrestricted permission to distribute copies to others.

Check your understanding

A developer publishes source with permission to redistribute it if notices remain. Does a recipient own the developer’s copyright, and may the notices be removed?

Check your answer

Publication and redistribution permission do not transfer ownership. Under the stated terms the recipient must retain the notices; permission to share is compatible with continuing copyright.

Free software and the Free Software Foundation

S7.05.A01

Explain what each freedom enables

The Free Software Foundation promotes users’ control over software. “Free” refers to freedom, not necessarily the price of a copy. Running for any purpose lets a user choose how to use the program; studying and changing it lets the user understand and adapt its behaviour. Redistribution lets others receive copies, while distributing modified versions lets others benefit from changes.

Connect rights to the source

Source code is the human-readable form used to understand and change the program. Supplying only an executable does not automatically provide a practical way to study and modify its source. The recipient needs access to source and the relevant permissions, not merely the technical ability to copy files. Lesson 030 explains source and translated forms.

Apply the definition to both prices

A business may charge for distributing free software or for maintenance and support. Receiving a paid copy is compatible with the freedoms if the licence preserves them. Equally, a no-charge download with no modification rights is not free software in this sense. Inspect permissions separately from cost.

Keep freedoms and obligations together

Exercise the freedoms under the licence’s conditions, such as preserving notices. Not every free-software licence imposes identical conditions on a distributed modification. The freedoms establish what users may do; the particular licence tells them how to comply when doing it.

Core explanation · Exam essentials

  • Free software concerns freedoms to run, study/change, redistribute copies and distribute modified versions.
  • Source access supports study and modification. A price does not by itself remove these freedoms.
Optional extension · Copyleft and more permissive conditions

This comparison explains continuing responsibilities, not a requirement to memorise licence texts. A copyleft licence can require distributed derivatives to preserve specified freedoms and provide corresponding source under its terms. A more permissive licence may allow different terms for derivatives while still requiring notices. Private modification and distribution are different acts; read the actual licence before inferring obligations.

Common misconception

Free software and free-of-charge software describe different properties.

Check your understanding

A developer charges for a copy released under a free-software licence. Is charging alone a contradiction?

Check your answer

No. The relevant question is whether the licence preserves the user's freedoms, not whether a payment is collected.

Open source and the Open Source Initiative

S7.05.A02

Check permission as well as visibility

The Open Source Initiative evaluates licences against its Open Source Definition. Access to source is necessary, but the distribution terms must also allow the required activities, including modification and redistribution. A vendor cannot establish open-source status merely by showing readable source while forbidding those activities.

Use counterexamples to locate the restriction

For example, a licence that permits reading source for inspection but forbids every change can help a user understand the code without granting the freedom to adapt it. A licence that forbids business use also conflicts with open-source criteria. These restrictions are different from an obligation to retain a copyright notice.

Explain the relationship between the approaches

Many licences satisfy both free-software and open-source definitions. FSF emphasises software freedom; OSI maintains open-source criteria and a licence approval process. Neither name identifies a single licence with one universal set of terms. When choosing software, state the permission required and check the offered licence rather than treating two organisation names as mutually exclusive products.

Core explanation · Exam essentials

  • The OSI maintains the Open Source Definition and approves licences that meet it.
  • Open source requires relevant use, modification and redistribution rights as well as source access; FSF and OSI are organisations, not individual licences.
Optional extension · Source-available and freeware are not permission guarantees

Source-available describes the ability to inspect source without establishing all open-source rights. Freeware usually describes no-charge distribution, with permissions still determined by its licence. These labels help diagnose a claim; the course does not require an exhaustive catalogue of software marketing categories.

Common misconception

An open-source licence does not mean the software has no owner or no conditions.

Check your understanding

A supplier publishes source but bans all redistribution. Is source visibility enough to call its licence open source?

Check your answer

No. Open-source criteria also require redistribution and modification rights; visibility by itself is insufficient.

04

Shareware: evaluate before continued use

S7.05.A03

Visual overview

Connect evaluation to a real requirement

A trial helps a user test suitability before committing to a purchase. The offer may limit time, features or permitted use. It is useful only if the trial exposes the feature the user needs to evaluate: an editor trial without scanner support cannot establish compatibility with the shop’s scanner.

Complete the trial-to-deployment decision

Read the offered terms, not a universal “30 days” rule. In the illustrated example, evaluation is allowed through day 30; after that period continued use requires the specified permission. A different offer might restrict saving or export until purchase. Check how to retain or export work before the trial ends.

Separate suitability from permission

A successful trial establishes evidence of suitability under tested conditions, not permission for indefinite use. If the required licence cannot be obtained, stop the use that the terms no longer allow and choose a permitted alternative. Do not assume trial distribution includes source, modification rights or unlimited copying.

Core explanation · Exam essentials

  • Shareware offers evaluation before payment for continued or complete use under the stated terms.
  • Check trial duration, available features, copying rights and what happens after the trial; trial access does not grant source-modification rights.
Common misconception

Shareware is not necessarily permanently free and should not be confused with free software.

Check your understanding

Why is a time-limited trial unsuitable as the sole basis for a year-long deployment?

Check your answer

The organisation needs permission covering the whole deployment, including continued use after the trial expires.

05

Commercial and proprietary software

S7.05.A04

Visual overview

Keep business model, price and permissions separate

A commercial supplier develops or distributes software as a business activity. Income may come from copies, subscriptions, maintenance or support. That business can use an open-source licence; a no-charge product can also be part of a commercial service. Commercial status and the price of a particular copy therefore answer different questions.

Read the actual restrictions

A proprietary licence commonly permits specified use while restricting modification or redistribution. Read which acts are allowed, how many people or devices are covered and how long permission lasts. Source may be unavailable or available under restrictive terms; source visibility alone does not settle whether the software is free or open source.

Distinguish a licence from a service commitment

Paid support is valuable only when the offer provides the needed service. Office-hours help is not a two-hour response at any time of day; a response commitment is not necessarily a promise to fix every fault within that time. Open-source software can also have a paid support agreement. Do not assume one licensing category always has better maintenance.

Connect the terms to the organisation

A suitable comparison includes total practical cost: purchase or subscription, configuration, staff expertise, maintenance and migration. Zero licence fees can coexist with substantial staff cost, while a paid product can still fail a required permission. Decide from the stated constraints rather than a preference for a label.

Core explanation · Exam essentials

  • Commercial describes business activity; proprietary describes restrictions on users’ freedoms. They are not synonyms.
  • Check users/devices, duration, permitted acts and contracted support. Price alone establishes none of these.
Common misconception

Do not infer proprietary restrictions solely from the word commercial; examine the actual licence.

Check your understanding

A company sells open-source software with an optional support contract. Can it still be commercial software?

Check your answer

Yes. Commercial concerns business activity, while open source concerns the rights granted by the licence.

Choose a licence from the requirements

S7.05.A05

Visual overview

Match a requirement to a licence feature

Deploy to a fixed teamEnough authorised seats and an appropriate term
Obtain help for critical workA support agreement covering the required service

Swipe horizontally to compare every column →

Turn needs into checkable requirements

Begin by listing what the organisation must do: run the program, adapt its source, distribute copies, authorise users or obtain a particular level of help. Distinguish a mandatory requirement from a preference. An option failing one mandatory requirement is not rescued by being cheapest.

Check all required acts

Compare evidence in the actual offers. If source modification is required, both suitable source access and permission to modify are needed. If a translated version will be sent to partners, check redistribution as a separate act. If no offer covers the required deployment, seek additional terms or another product instead of pretending that a category name grants missing rights.

Follow the decision into use

After choosing, state what must continue: retain specified notices, keep enough authorised seats, renew time-limited rights or fund maintenance. A licence choice is complete when it covers the intended activity and its obligations, not when a familiar name has been selected.

Worked example · Compare three offers for ten community centres

  1. Requirements

    A charity has programmers. It must translate a tool’s interface, distribute the changed version to ten centres and pay no licence fees. Staff maintenance time is available.

  2. Offer A

    No-charge open-source software supplies source and permits use, modification and redistribution if notices are retained. No contracted support response is included.

  3. Offers B and C

    B is a no-charge 14-day trial with no source or modification rights. C is a paid one-centre proprietary licence forbidding redistribution.

  4. Decision

    Choose A: source access and modification rights cover translation; redistribution covers all ten centres; zero licence fees meet the budget.

  5. Why not B or C?

    B cannot authorise the translation and continued deployment. C fails both the budget and redistribution requirements; one-centre permission is insufficient.

  6. Complete deployment

    Retain A’s required notices in the distributed versions and allocate staff time to maintain the adaptation. The absence of a licence charge does not remove this work.

Worked example · Change the requirement: a repair service needs dependable support

  1. Requirements

    A repair service needs four unmodified installations for two years and a support response within four hours during every operating day. It can afford either offer.

  2. Offers

    A supplies source and unrestricted installations with community help but no response commitment. B permits four installations for the full two years and includes the required contracted response; it forbids modification and redistribution.

  3. Decision

    Choose B under these conditions because its specified installation term and support commitment meet the requirements. Source modification is not needed. A’s permissions do not supply its missing service commitment.

  4. Trade-off and check

    The service accepts B’s payment and restrictions. Confirm which faults and operating hours the support contract covers; a response is not necessarily a completed repair.

  5. Change one condition

    If the service now must adapt source and share its version with partners, B’s present terms fail. Seek a different agreement or compare A with a suitable paid support contract. Do not reuse the previous conclusion unchanged.

Core explanation · Exam essentials

  • Translate the scenario into required acts, then match each to an explicit licence or support term.
  • Justify the choice, reject unsuitable alternatives and state continuing obligations and practical limits.
Common misconception

A licence choice needs the supplied scenario and terms, not a claim that one category is always cheapest or safest.

Check your understanding

If a school only needs unmodified software with guaranteed response times, must it choose open source?

Check your answer

No. Compare the actual installation rights, cost and support agreement; source modification may not be a requirement.

Question 1 · Command word: Explain2 teacher marks

Explain two reasons why copyright legislation is needed for software that takes a year to develop but can be copied in seconds.

S7.04.A01
Answer and checking points — teacher-written
  1. Enforceable rights allow the creator to challenge unauthorised copies despite the low cost of making them.
  2. Control over permitted distribution gives the creator a way to obtain revenue to support the development investment.

Common error: Develop the connection between copying, ownership and investment.

Question 2 · Command word: Describe4 teacher marks

Describe the four freedoms associated with the FSF's free-software approach and state which need source access.

S7.05.A01
Answer and checking points — teacher-written
  1. Users may run the software for any purpose.
  2. Users may study and change how it works; this requires source code.
  3. Users may redistribute copies.
  4. Users may distribute modified versions; producing meaningful source modifications requires source access.

Common error: Freedom concerns permitted actions, not a promise that every copy costs nothing.

Question 3 · Command word: Explain2 teacher marks

Explain the role of the OSI and why publishing readable source while prohibiting all modifications does not establish open-source licensing.

S7.05.A02
Answer and checking points — teacher-written
  1. The OSI assesses licences against its Open Source Definition and approves those meeting the criteria.
  2. A licence prohibiting all modifications withholds a required open-source permission even if the source can be read.

Common error: Distinguish a licence's permissions from the visibility of a file.

Question 4 · Command word: Explain2 teacher marks

A company sells an open-source program, while another gives away a closed-source utility. Explain why price alone does not distinguish open-source and proprietary licensing.

S7.05.A02S7.05.A04
Answer and checking points — teacher-written
  1. The sold program may retain modification and redistribution permissions, so a price does not remove its open-source status.
  2. The no-charge utility may still restrict changes and copying, so zero price does not grant open-source rights.

Common error: Compare permissions, not just payment.

Question 5 · Command word: State3 teacher marks

A proprietary licence covers five named users for one year, with support only during office hours. State three terms a manager should check before assigning it to eight users who need round-the-clock help for two years.

S7.05.A04
Answer and checking points — teacher-written
  1. Increase or change the permission to cover all eight users.
  2. Obtain a term or renewal arrangement covering two years.
  3. Arrange support availability that covers the required hours.

Common error: Use the stated licence constraints; do not assume payment covers every deployment.

Question 6 · Command word: Justify3 teacher marks

A volunteer group must modify a program and share it with partner groups. Offer A permits both acts if copyright notices are retained. Offer B permits use only and supplies no source. Justify one choice and state an obligation.

S7.05.A05
Answer and checking points — teacher-written
  1. Choose A because its modification rights allow the group's required changes.
  2. Its redistribution rights allow partner groups to receive the adapted copies.
  3. Keep the specified copyright notices when distributing the software.

Common error: A valid justification uses the offered terms rather than a category label alone.

Question 7 · Command word: Justify2 teacher marks

A shop is unsure whether a stock-control program works with its scanner. Its shareware trial includes scanner support for 30 days, and the shop can pay for continued use. Justify using the trial before purchase and explain one limitation.

S7.05.A03S7.05.A05
Answer and checking points — teacher-written
  1. Testing the supplied scanner support can establish compatibility before the shop pays.
  2. The trial only authorises the stated period, so a successful test still needs a licence purchase for later operation.

Common error: The scenario makes evaluation useful but does not establish indefinite free use.

Question 8 · Command word: Justify4 teacher marks

An archive needs software for twelve staff for three years. It must not modify source, but requires a support response within one working day. Offer A permits all installations at no charge with community help and no response commitment. Offer B covers twelve staff for three years and contracts the required response; its cost fits the budget and it forbids redistribution. Justify a choice and one trade-off. Later the archive must distribute an adapted version to partner archives: explain how that change affects the choice and one next step.

S7.05.A04S7.05.A05
Answer and checking points — teacher-written
  1. Choose B initially: its named-user term and contracted response meet the supplied requirements, whereas A does not promise the required response.
  2. The archive accepts B’s affordable cost and redistribution restriction in exchange for the required service; redistribution and source modification are not initially needed.
  3. The later adaptation/distribution requirement is not covered by B’s current permissions, so the original recommendation cannot simply be retained.
  4. Seek suitable changed permissions or compare another product, such as software with explicit modification/redistribution rights and a separate support agreement meeting the response requirement.

Common error: Do not infer modification rights from permission to install, or that community help guarantees a response time.

Original question · Cambridge 9618/12

1. Choose a software licence and explain copyright

Cambridge 9618/12 · October/November 2024 · 5(b)(i), 5(b)(ii) · 5 marks

Selected parts: 5(b)(i) [3]; 5(b)(ii) [2].

Reading the question — teacher guidance

The business can modify source code and pay for continuing maintenance. Consider both conditions: payment does not automatically mean closed source. Then answer the separate copyright question.

Solution / model answer — teacher-written

5(b)(i): An open-source/free-software licence is suitable: it permits access to and modification of source code, while the programmer can charge for maintenance or updates.

5(b)(ii): Copyright identifies the programmer’s ownership of the work and provides a basis for action against copying or distribution that breaches the permitted terms.

Official mark scheme
Why this earns marks — teacher explanation

Part (i) awards one mark for the licence category and up to two for reasons. The official scheme uses “Open Source Initiative // Free Software Foundation” for its licence answer. Those are organisation names, not names of individual licences; the intended category permits source access and modification. Part (ii) has a separate two-mark cap.

Common mistakes — teacher analysis

Free software does not necessarily mean no money can be charged. Copyright and an open licence can coexist: the licence states what the copyright holder permits.
