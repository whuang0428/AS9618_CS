Copyright and software licences

Explain copyright protection, distinguish software licensing approaches and choose a licence from stated permissions and requirements.

Which rights does a user need, and which licence actually grants them?

Before you start

A program is available to download without payment. Does that establish permission to change and redistribute its source?

Check your answer

No. Price does not establish those permissions; inspect the licence terms and source availability.

01

Why software needs copyright protection

S7.04.A01

Visual overview

Core explanation

Writing software takes time, skill and investment, while copying a finished program can cost very little. Copyright legislation gives the rights holder enforceable control over protected expression such as original source code and documentation. This allows unauthorised copying or distribution to be challenged and enables creators to grant permissions and obtain revenue from their work.

Copyright protects the expression of a program rather than every underlying idea or mathematical method. A licence grants permission for specified acts under conditions; buying a copy normally does not transfer ownership of its copyright. A user should establish the applicable permissions before copying, modifying or redistributing software, taking account of any relevant legal exceptions.

Worked example · A purchased program copied to a class

  1. Situation

    A tutor buys software whose licence permits installation on one machine. The tutor wants to copy it to twenty classroom computers.

  2. Read the permission

    The payment obtained a one-machine permission; it did not transfer the source code's copyright or grant twenty installations.

  3. Decision

    Obtain suitable classroom permissions or select another tool whose licence permits the intended installations.

  4. Reason

    Cheap copying does not remove the creator's enforceable rights or enlarge the licence that was purchased.

Common misconception

Permission to download one copy is not unrestricted permission to distribute copies to others.

Check your understanding

Why can a licence allow copying without ending copyright protection?

Check your answer

The rights holder can grant particular permissions while retaining ownership and enforcing the licence's conditions.

Free software and the Free Software Foundation

S7.05.A01

Visual overview

Four freedoms in use

RedistributeShare copies with other users
Share modified versionsDistribute improvements subject to the licence

Swipe horizontally to compare every column →

Core explanation

The Free Software Foundation (FSF) promotes software freedom. Its approach gives users freedoms to run a program for any purpose, study and change it, redistribute copies, and distribute modified versions. Access to source code is necessary for studying how the program works and making useful changes.

Free refers to these freedoms, not necessarily a zero price. A copy or support service can be sold while the user retains the freedoms granted by a free-software licence. Some licences use copyleft conditions to preserve freedoms in distributed derivatives; not every free-software licence imposes the same redistribution conditions.

Common misconception

Free software and free-of-charge software describe different properties.

Check your understanding

A developer charges for a copy released under a free-software licence. Is charging alone a contradiction?

Check your answer

No. The relevant question is whether the licence preserves the user's freedoms, not whether a payment is collected.

Open source and the Open Source Initiative

S7.05.A02

Visual overview

Use, modify and share source under an approved licenceOpen-source permissions with continuing obligations
Sell copies while preserving licence termsCharging does not itself prevent open-source distribution

Swipe horizontally to compare every column →

Core explanation

The Open Source Initiative (OSI) maintains the Open Source Definition and approves licences that satisfy its criteria. Open-source licensing includes access to source code and permission to modify and redistribute the software. Merely allowing users to look at source code is insufficient if the licence prohibits the necessary uses or redistribution.

FSF and OSI are organisations, not two individual licences. Many licences satisfy both free-software and open-source criteria. Their emphasis differs: the FSF stresses users' freedoms, while the OSI evaluates licences against open-source criteria. For a real choice, read the licence's obligations, such as keeping notices or conditions on distributing modified work.

Common misconception

An open-source licence does not mean the software has no owner or no conditions.

Check your understanding

A supplier publishes source but bans all redistribution. Is source visibility enough to call its licence open source?

Check your answer

No. Open-source criteria also require redistribution and modification rights; visibility by itself is insufficient.

04

Shareware: evaluate before continued use

S7.05.A03

Visual overview

Core explanation

Shareware is distributed so users can try software before paying for continued or complete use. A trial may expire after a stated period or restrict features until a licence is purchased. The exact trial terms determine what use and copying are permitted.

This approach can reduce the risk of buying an unsuitable program because a user can test it on their own system. However, a successful trial is not a permanent entitlement, and shareware does not automatically include source code or permission to modify it. Budget for any required payment and check what happens when the trial ends.

Common misconception

Shareware is not necessarily permanently free and should not be confused with free software.

Check your understanding

Why is a time-limited trial unsuitable as the sole basis for a year-long deployment?

Check your answer

The organisation needs permission covering the whole deployment, including continued use after the trial expires.

05

Commercial and proprietary software

S7.05.A04

Visual overview

Core explanation

Commercial software is developed or supplied as a business activity. A supplier may earn income from licences, subscriptions, support or other services. Commercial and proprietary are not synonyms: a business can sell free or open-source software, while proprietary software can sometimes be offered without payment.

A proprietary licence commonly permits specified use while limiting copying, modification or redistribution. Terms may specify the number of users or devices, duration and support arrangements. Paid support can be useful when it is actually included in the agreement; payment alone does not guarantee support, source access or unrestricted installation.

Common misconception

Do not infer proprietary restrictions solely from the word commercial; examine the actual licence.

Check your understanding

A company sells open-source software with an optional support contract. Can it still be commercial software?

Check your answer

Yes. Commercial concerns business activity, while open source concerns the rights granted by the licence.

06

Choose a licence from the requirements

S7.05.A05

Visual overview

Match a requirement to a licence feature

Required activityEvidence to seek
Adapt source and share the adaptationModification and redistribution permissions plus applicable conditions
Evaluate an unfamiliar toolTrial duration and available features
Deploy to a fixed teamEnough authorised seats and an appropriate term
Obtain help for critical workA support agreement covering the required service

Swipe horizontally to compare every column →

Core explanation

Identify the organisation's required acts before selecting software: running it on the intended devices, modifying source, distributing copies or obtaining support. Then compare those needs with the permissions, costs and obligations of the offered licences. A cheap option is unsuitable if its terms prohibit a required act.

A justification connects a stated requirement to a licence feature and its consequence. Check practical limits as well: source modification needs skilled staff, a trial needs a continuation plan, and supplier support needs agreed scope and availability. There is no single licence category that is best for every organisation.

Worked example · Choose a tool for community centres

  1. Requirements

    A charity has programmers, needs to translate a tool and distribute the translated version to ten centres, and has no budget for licence fees.

  2. Offers

    A: no-charge open-source software allowing modification and redistribution if notices are preserved. B: a free 14-day trial with no source access. C: a paid, one-centre proprietary licence forbidding redistribution.

  3. Choice

    Choose A: its permissions cover translation and distribution, and the licence price fits the budget.

  4. Obligation and limit

    Preserve the required notices in distributed copies and allocate staff time for maintenance; no-charge licensing does not eliminate development costs.

Common misconception

A licence choice needs the supplied scenario and terms, not a claim that one category is always cheapest or safest.

Check your understanding

If a school only needs unmodified software with guaranteed response times, must it choose open source?

Check your answer

No. Compare the actual installation rights, cost and support agreement; source modification may not be a requirement.

Cambridge command words

Practice questions

Question 1 · Command word: Explain2 marks

Explain two reasons why copyright legislation is needed for software that takes a year to develop but can be copied in seconds.

S7.04.A01
Answer and marking points
  1. Enforceable rights allow the creator to challenge unauthorised copies despite the low cost of making them.
  2. Control over permitted distribution gives the creator a way to obtain revenue to support the development investment.

Common error: Develop the connection between copying, ownership and investment.

Question 2 · Command word: Describe4 marks

Describe the four freedoms associated with the FSF's free-software approach and state which need source access.

S7.05.A01
Answer and marking points
  1. Users may run the software for any purpose.
  2. Users may study and change how it works; this requires source code.
  3. Users may redistribute copies.
  4. Users may distribute modified versions; producing meaningful source modifications requires source access.

Common error: Freedom concerns permitted actions, not a promise that every copy costs nothing.

Question 3 · Command word: Explain2 marks

Explain the role of the OSI and why publishing readable source while prohibiting all modifications does not establish open-source licensing.

S7.05.A02
Answer and marking points
  1. The OSI assesses licences against its Open Source Definition and approves those meeting the criteria.
  2. A licence prohibiting all modifications withholds a required open-source permission even if the source can be read.

Common error: Distinguish a licence's permissions from the visibility of a file.

Question 4 · Command word: Explain2 marks

A shareware editor stops saving documents after a 21-day trial unless a licence is bought. Explain one benefit of the trial and one action needed for continued full use.

S7.05.A03
Answer and marking points
  1. The user can test whether the editor suits their work before committing to a purchase.
  2. The user must obtain the stated paid permission to keep the full saving functionality after the trial.

Common error: Do not describe this time-limited offer as permanently free software.

Question 5 · Command word: Explain2 marks

A company sells an open-source program, while another gives away a closed-source utility. Explain why price alone does not distinguish open-source and proprietary licensing.

S7.05.A02S7.05.A04
Answer and marking points
  1. The sold program may retain modification and redistribution permissions, so a price does not remove its open-source status.
  2. The no-charge utility may still restrict changes and copying, so zero price does not grant open-source rights.

Common error: Compare permissions, not just payment.

Question 6 · Command word: State3 marks

A proprietary licence covers five named users for one year, with support only during office hours. State three terms a manager should check before assigning it to eight users who need round-the-clock help for two years.

S7.05.A04
Answer and marking points
  1. Increase or change the permission to cover all eight users.
  2. Obtain a term or renewal arrangement covering two years.
  3. Arrange support availability that covers the required hours.

Common error: Use the stated licence constraints; do not assume payment covers every deployment.

Question 7 · Command word: Justify3 marks

A volunteer group must modify a program and share it with partner groups. Offer A permits both acts if copyright notices are retained. Offer B permits use only and supplies no source. Justify one choice and state an obligation.

S7.05.A05
Answer and marking points
  1. Choose A because its modification rights allow the group's required changes.
  2. Its redistribution rights allow partner groups to receive the adapted copies.
  3. Keep the specified copyright notices when distributing the software.

Common error: A valid justification uses the offered terms rather than a category label alone.

Question 8 · Command word: Justify2 marks

A shop is unsure whether a stock-control program works with its scanner. Its shareware trial includes scanner support for 30 days, and the shop can pay for continued use. Justify using the trial before purchase and explain one limitation.

S7.05.A03S7.05.A05
Answer and marking points
  1. Testing the supplied scanner support can establish compatibility before the shop pays.
  2. The trial only authorises the stated period, so a successful test still needs a licence purchase for later operation.

Common error: The scenario makes evaluation useful but does not establish indefinite free use.

Question 1 · Command word: Explain · 4 marks

An independent developer spends months creating a simulation. A training firm copies it to its customers without permission, arguing that electronic copies cost almost nothing. Explain why copyright legislation is needed here and why buying one copy would not normally settle the firm's distribution rights.

Answer and marking points
  • The low copying cost enables others to distribute the developer's expression without funding the work that produced it.
  • Copyright supplies a legal basis for controlling and challenging that unauthorised distribution.
  • Control of licensed distribution can help the developer receive a return on the simulation's creation.
  • Purchasing a copy generally grants specified use rather than ownership or unrestricted rights to distribute customer copies.

Common error: Do not claim copyright gives ownership of every scientific idea used by the simulation.

Question 2 · Command word: Describe · 4 marks

Describe how FSF and OSI relate to software licensing. A supplier calls a 10-day, closed-source trial 'free software because it is commercial shareware supplied at zero price'. Explain two errors in that reasoning.

Answer and marking points
  • The FSF promotes licences that preserve users' freedoms to run, study, change and share programs.
  • The OSI maintains open-source criteria and recognises licences that comply with them.
  • A temporary zero-price offer does not establish the freedoms associated with free software, particularly when source access and change rights are absent.
  • Commercial describes business activity and shareware describes trial distribution; neither label proves free-software permissions, and continued use may need payment.

Common error: FSF and OSI are organisations; the supplier's licence terms determine what the user may do.

Question 3 · Command word: Justify · 4 marks

A clinic needs software for six receptionists for three years. It will not modify source and requires a two-hour support response. Offer A is no-charge open-source software with community help but no response commitment. Offer B is a proprietary subscription covering six users and contracted two-hour support. The clinic can afford B for all three years. Justify a choice, explain one trade-off and state a licence term to confirm.

Answer and marking points
  • B meets the clinic's stated support response requirement through a contract, unlike the uncommitted community help in A.
  • Its six-user permission matches the reception team and the given budget supports the planned subscription period.
  • The clinic accepts subscription expenditure and source restrictions in exchange for a service it needs; modification rights are not required here.
  • Confirm that the subscription and support coverage remain valid throughout all three years and cover the intended use.

Common error: Choosing open source solely because it is free to obtain does not address the required support commitment.

Lesson review

Summary

Copyright and permission

Rights in software expression remain distinct from the acts a particular licence permits.

FSF and OSI

User freedoms and open-source criteria overlap across many licences; source visibility alone is insufficient.

Shareware

Evaluate under stated trial terms, then obtain the rights needed for continued use.

Commercial

Business activity can use open-source or proprietary licences; price alone does not determine permissions.

Selection

Match required use, changes, distribution and support to actual terms and obligations.
