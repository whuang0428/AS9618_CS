Ethics and ownership · Concept 01

Separate owning software rights from using a copy

Observe

Look, then discuss

If the school buys one copy, can it automatically change the code and send copies to every partner school?

The programmer has spent months writing the assistant. Copying the completed files takes seconds. Consider who controls the work and what a school actually receives when it downloads or buys a copy.

Recall

Source code and a program copy

Source code is the human-readable form written by programmers. An executable is a form used to run the program. Possessing a file, being able to read source and having permission to change or share it are separate matters.

Explain

Explanation · 1 / 4

Explain why protection is needed

Software requires skill, time and investment to create, while finished copies can be reproduced cheaply. Copyright legislation gives a basis for controlling restricted copying and distribution and for acting against unauthorised use of protected work. This supports creators in deciding the terms on which their work is made available.

Explanation · 2 / 4

Identify ownership and permission

The copyright holder controls relevant rights in the protected software expression. A licence grants permission to perform specified acts under stated conditions. Buying a copy normally grants the licensed use; it does not automatically transfer ownership of the copyright or every right to modify and distribute the program.

Explanation · 3 / 4

Check each intended activity

Running a program, modifying its source and redistributing copies are different activities. A licence can permit one and restrict another. For the school, permission to run an assistant on one device does not answer whether a modified version may be supplied to ten other schools.

Explanation · 4 / 4

Understand how openness and copyright coexist

A copyright holder can grant broad permissions while requiring notices to be retained. Publishing source therefore does not automatically remove copyright. Protection concerns the program’s expression; do not treat it as ownership of every general idea that another programmer might implement independently.

Work together

Read a short set of offered terms

A fictional licence permits use on two school computers and redistribution of unchanged copies if the copyright notice remains. It does not grant permission to modify the source.

Work together · 1 / 2

Separate the acts

Running on two computers is within the stated permission. Redistributing an unchanged copy must retain the notice. Modifying the source requires additional permission under this offer.

Work together · 2 / 2

Explain what stays with the owner

The recipient has the stated permissions, while the rights holder retains copyright. The school cannot remove the notice merely because it paid for a copy or is allowed to share it.

A licence states permitted acts and conditions; receiving a copy does not automatically transfer copyright.

Check

Explain it yourself · Teacher-written

A programmer allows anyone to share unchanged copies while retaining a copyright notice. Is that permission inconsistent with copyright protection?

Check your reasoning

No. The copyright holder is using their rights to grant permission subject to conditions. Sharing within those terms is authorised; the permission does not transfer copyright or authorise removing the notice.

Connect

Keep this idea

Explain why creators need protection, then check which acts the licence permits.

Some licences grant broad user freedoms, including studying, changing and sharing software.

Check a common misunderstanding

Technical ability to copy a file is not the same as permission to redistribute it.

Learning goals
  • Explain why copyright legislation is needed for software.

Understand what “free” means in free software

Look, then discuss

Can software cost money and still be free software?

Offer A costs nothing but forbids source modification. Offer B charges for a copy and support while allowing users to run, study, change and share it under its terms. Decide which information concerns price and which concerns freedom.

Explain

Explanation · 1 / 4

Meet the Free Software Foundation

The Free Software Foundation, or FSF, promotes software freedom. In its approach, “free” concerns what users are permitted to do, not necessarily whether they pay for a copy. FSF is an organisation; an individual software licence supplies the actual terms.

Explanation · 2 / 4

Explain the four freedoms through the school’s actions

The school can run the program for any purpose. It can study how the program works and change it to meet its needs. It can redistribute copies to others. It can distribute modified versions so others benefit from the changes. Study and modification require access to the source.

Explanation · 3 / 4

Connect source access to practical control

A downloadable executable lets users run a program, but does not by itself provide its human-readable source. The school needs both source access and the relevant permission to make an authorised source change. Possessing one without the other does not meet that requirement.

Explanation · 4 / 4

Separate charging from restricting freedom

A supplier can charge for distribution, maintenance or support while preserving software freedoms. Conversely, a no-charge download may restrict modification or redistribution. Users must still follow the licence’s conditions, such as retaining notices; free software is compatible with continuing obligations.

Work together

Pay for support and keep the freedoms

The school pays a supplier to maintain an assistant whose licence grants the four freedoms. The school receives the source and plans to add a new interface language.

Work together · 1 / 2

Check permission rather than price

The supplied source and modification rights allow the school to study and change the interface. The support fee does not remove those permissions.

Work together · 2 / 2

Complete the planned sharing

Check the conditions for distributing the modified version and retain required notices. The school can exercise its freedoms while complying with these obligations and paying for maintenance.

“Free software” describes user freedoms. The price of a copy or support agreement is a separate question.

Check

Explain it yourself · Teacher-written

A no-charge assistant supplies only an executable and forbids changes or redistribution. Does “no charge” establish that it is free software in the FSF sense?

Check your reasoning

No. The stated restrictions do not provide the required freedoms, and source access needed for study and modification is missing. The absence of a price establishes only the cost of obtaining this copy.

Connect

Keep this idea

Free software protects freedoms to run, study and change, redistribute copies and distribute modified versions.

Open source also concerns source and permissions. We need to distinguish its criteria from simply seeing the code.

Check a common misunderstanding

Free software does not mean that programmers cannot charge for copies or services.

Learning goals
  • Explain free software in the Free Software Foundation approach.

Ethics and ownership · Concept 03

Check permissions as well as visible source

Observe

Look, then discuss

A website shows the assistant’s source but forbids every modification. Is seeing the code enough?

Compare an offer that permits inspection only with an offer permitting source modification and redistribution. Both let the school read the code, but only one supports adapting and sharing the assistant.

Explain

Explanation · 1 / 4

Meet the Open Source Initiative

The Open Source Initiative, or OSI, maintains the Open Source Definition and approves licences that meet it. Open-source software makes source available under terms that provide the required permissions, including modification and redistribution. The OSI is an organisation, not the name of one universal licence.

Explanation · 2 / 4

Distinguish visibility from permission

A source-visible product may allow inspection while forbidding changes or sharing. Inspection helps someone understand the code, but those restrictions do not provide the necessary open-source freedoms. Read the distribution terms as well as checking whether a source download exists.

Explanation · 3 / 4

Use a counterexample to test the label

If an offer permits modification for school use but prohibits all business use, it restricts a field of endeavour and does not meet open-source criteria. Conditions such as preserving copyright notices are different: a licence can impose such conditions while allowing the required activities.

Explanation · 4 / 4

Relate FSF and OSI without confusing them

Many licences satisfy both free-software and open-source definitions. The FSF emphasises users’ freedoms; the OSI maintains open-source criteria and a licence approval process. These are overlapping approaches with distinct organisations. For a concrete choice, identify the permissions the user needs and check the actual licence.

Work together

Compare two source downloads

Offer A supplies readable source for inspection but prohibits modification and redistribution. Offer B supplies source and permits use, modification and redistribution while requiring notices to remain.

Work together · 1 / 2

Test the school’s modification requirement

A fails because viewing the source does not grant the right to change it. B supplies the relevant source and modification permission.

Work together · 2 / 2

Test the sharing requirement

A also fails the plan to send copies to partner schools. B permits that activity under its notice condition. These facts explain the choice more precisely than saying that both products have “available code”.

Source access is necessary for source adaptation, but the licence must also permit the intended acts.

Check

Explain it yourself · Teacher-written

An exam scheme uses “Open Source Initiative” as shorthand in a licence answer. How should you explain the permission the school actually requires?

Check your reasoning

Explain that an open-source licence should provide source access and permit modification and the required redistribution under its terms. OSI is the organisation associated with the criteria; it is not an individual licence granting rights by name alone.

Connect

Keep this idea

Open source combines source access with the required permissions; check terms rather than visibility alone.

We can now compare these permissions with a trial offer and with a paid business arrangement.

Check a common misunderstanding

FSF and OSI are not mutually exclusive software products, and neither name determines every condition of a licence.

Learning goals
  • Explain the role of the Open Source Initiative and open-source permissions.

Ethics and ownership · Concept 04

Separate a trial, a price and a business model

Observe

Look, then discuss

What does a successful free trial tell the school, and what does it leave unanswered?

The school tests an assistant for 30 days. It works on the school’s camera, but the terms require payment for continued use and do not permit source modification. Compare the evidence that the software works with the permissions required for next term.

Explain

Explanation · 1 / 5

Explain shareware as evaluation under terms

Shareware lets a user evaluate software before paying for continued or complete use. An offer may limit time, features or other uses. Thirty days is the condition in this example, not a universal rule. Users benefit by checking suitability before purchase; developers can use trial feedback to improve the software and may gain users through recommendations.

Explanation · 2 / 5

Complete the trial decision

The trial supplies evidence of suitability under the tested conditions. It does not authorise unlimited later use. Before the permitted evaluation ends, obtain appropriate rights for the planned use or stop using the restricted offer. Check whether work can be exported and whether the trial includes the relevant permissions.

Explanation · 3 / 5

Distinguish commercial from proprietary

Commercial software is developed or distributed as a business activity. A business may earn money from copies, subscriptions, maintenance or support, including services for open-source software. Proprietary software restricts users’ freedoms, commonly limiting modification or redistribution. A no-charge proprietary product is possible, as is a paid open-source product.

Explanation · 4 / 5

Explain typical commercial benefits accurately

Charging for software can provide income to fund development. Users can benefit from support that helps resolve difficulties, and commercial products are often tested before distribution. These describe possible or usual benefits, not guarantees: check the offered support and testing evidence. The business model alone does not prove that a particular product is reliable.

Explanation · 5 / 5

Read deployment and support terms separately

Check how many users or devices are covered, how long permission lasts and which acts are allowed. Then check the service agreement. A promised response within four hours does not necessarily mean a completed repair within four hours. Paying for a copy does not by itself establish either source access or a particular quality of support.

Work together

Change from trial to school-wide use

The assistant passes a 30-day evaluation on one device. The school now needs 20 devices for a year, a translated interface and next-day support.

Work together · 1 / 2

List what the trial established

It provides evidence about the tested camera and features on the trial device. It does not establish permission for 20 devices, one year of use, a source change or a support response.

Work together · 2 / 2

Check the missing terms

Obtain or compare offers covering each requirement. If the current proprietary offer forbids modification, its successful trial cannot solve the interface requirement. A suitable alternative or changed agreement is needed.

Suitability, permission, price and support are separate parts of the decision.

Check

Explain it yourself · Teacher-written

A business gives away an open-source assistant and charges schools for maintenance. Can the activity be commercial even though the download is free of charge?

Check your reasoning

Yes. The business earns income from maintenance, so commercial activity does not require charging for each copy. The open-source licence still determines user permissions, while the maintenance contract determines the paid service.

Original question · Cambridge 9618/11

1. Explain the benefits of trial and commercial distribution

Cambridge 9618/11 · October/November 2024 · 7(a), 7(b) · 4 marks

Selected parts: 7(a) [2]; 7(b) [2].

Hint — reading the question

Treat the two parts separately. For each licence model, give two different benefits and identify who benefits: the user, the developer, or both. A shareware trial can help someone decide whether the program suits their needs.

Reasoning and model answer — teacher-written
PartTwo suitable benefits
7(a): sharewareUsers can try the program before purchase to check whether it meets their needs. The developer can use trial feedback to improve the program.
7(b): commercialThe developer can earn a fee from distributing the software. Support is usually available to help users resolve problems.
Official mark scheme
Why this earns marks — teacher explanation

The official scheme awards one mark per accepted point, up to two for each part. For shareware it also accepts no initial cost or users promoting the software to others. For commercial distribution it also accepts software usually being well tested.

The scheme describes typical benefits; it does not establish that every commercial product is well tested or that every licence includes support. Commercial activity can also use an open-source licence. Check the actual offer when making a real choice.

Common mistakes — teacher analysis

Repeating that a trial lets the user test the program and decide whether it suits them gives one benefit, not two. Do not claim that all trials are unlimited, that payment transfers copyright, or that commercial distribution always removes source-code rights.

Ethics and ownership · Concept 05

Match the complete plan to the offered terms

Observe

Look, then discuss

Which offer actually permits the school to adapt the assistant and share it?

The school has programmers, needs to translate the interface and distribute its version to ten partner schools, and has no budget for licence fees. It can allocate staff time for maintenance. Treat each of those statements as a condition to check.

Explain

Explanation · 1 / 4

Turn the plan into required acts

The school must run the assistant, inspect and modify its source, and distribute modified copies. Zero licence fees are a financial constraint. Available maintenance staff are a practical resource. Separate these from a preference such as liking a familiar product name.

Explanation · 2 / 4

Check every mandatory requirement

An option that fails a mandatory permission is unsuitable for the stated plan even if it is cheap. Modification requires source access and permission. Distribution to partners is a further act that needs permission. A licence covering one school cannot be silently treated as covering ten.

Explanation · 3 / 4

Account for obligations and maintenance

A suitable licence can require notices to remain or impose conditions when modified copies are distributed. Follow those terms. Also allocate time and skill to maintain the adaptation; absence of a licence fee does not remove integration and support work.

Explanation · 4 / 4

Revisit the choice when the requirements change

A school needing an unmodified tool and a guaranteed support response may favour a different offer from one needing extensive source adaptation. Justify the choice using the actual terms. Open-source permissions do not automatically supply a support service, and a support contract does not automatically supply modification rights.

Work together

Three offers for ten partner schools

These are fictional teaching offers. A supplies source and permits use, modification and redistribution at no licence charge if notices remain. B is a no-charge 14-day trial without source or modification rights. C is a paid one-school proprietary licence that forbids redistribution.

Work together · 1 / 4

Choose from requirements

A meets the required acts: its source can be translated, and its modified version can be shared with ten schools under the notice condition. It also meets the no-licence-fee constraint.

Work together · 2 / 4

Explain why the alternatives fail

B does not permit the planned adaptation or continuing deployment. C fails the budget and the distribution requirement. Neither failure is repaired merely by the software working well in a demonstration.

Work together · 3 / 4

State the continuing responsibility

Retain the required notices and allocate the available staff to maintain the school’s changes. Verify that the distributed package complies with the stated terms.

Work together · 4 / 4

Change one condition

Suppose the school no longer needs adaptation or sharing, but requires a four-hour support response. A’s community support alone supplies no such commitment. Compare a paid support agreement for A with another licence and service offer that covers the new requirements.

Choose from required permissions, practical constraints and actual service terms, then explain continuing obligations.

Check

Explain it yourself · Teacher-written

A suitable licence allows modification, but the school now wants to distribute its modified version. What additional checks are needed?

Check your reasoning

Check permission to redistribute the modified version and its conditions, including any required notices or source provision under the actual terms. Permission to modify alone does not establish every distribution right. Also check that all intended recipients and uses are covered.

Original question · Cambridge 9618/12

1. Choose a software licence and explain copyright

Cambridge 9618/12 · October/November 2024 · 5(b)(i), 5(b)(ii) · 5 marks

Selected parts: 5(b)(i) [3]; 5(b)(ii) [2].

Hint — reading the question

The business can modify source code and pay for continuing maintenance. Consider both conditions: payment does not automatically mean closed source. Then answer the separate copyright question.

Reasoning and model answer — teacher-written

5(b)(i): An open-source/free-software licence is suitable: it permits access to and modification of source code, while the programmer can charge for maintenance or updates.

5(b)(ii): Copyright identifies the programmer’s ownership of the work and provides a basis for action against copying or distribution that breaches the permitted terms.

Official mark scheme
Why this earns marks — teacher explanation

Part (i) awards one mark for the licence category and up to two for reasons. The official scheme uses “Open Source Initiative // Free Software Foundation” for its licence answer. Those are organisation names, not names of individual licences; the intended category permits source access and modification. Part (ii) has a separate two-mark cap.

Common mistakes — teacher analysis

Free software does not necessarily mean no money can be charged. Copyright and an open licence can coexist: the licence states what the copyright holder permits.

Question 1 · Command word: Explain2 teacher marks

Explain two reasons why copyright legislation is needed for software that takes a year to develop but can be copied in seconds.

S7.04.A01
Answer and checking points — teacher-written
  1. Enforceable rights allow the creator to challenge unauthorised copies despite the low cost of making them.
  2. Control over permitted distribution gives the creator a way to obtain revenue to support the development investment.

Common error: Develop the connection between copying, ownership and investment.

Question 2 · Command word: Describe4 teacher marks

Describe the four freedoms associated with the FSF's free-software approach and state which need source access.

S7.05.A01
Answer and checking points — teacher-written
  1. Users may run the software for any purpose.
  2. Users may study and change how it works; this requires source code.
  3. Users may redistribute copies.
  4. Users may distribute modified versions; producing meaningful source modifications requires source access.

Common error: Freedom concerns permitted actions, not a promise that every copy costs nothing.

Question 3 · Command word: Explain2 teacher marks

Explain the role of the OSI and why publishing readable source while prohibiting all modifications does not establish open-source licensing.

S7.05.A02
Answer and checking points — teacher-written
  1. The OSI assesses licences against its Open Source Definition and approves those meeting the criteria.
  2. A licence prohibiting all modifications withholds a required open-source permission even if the source can be read.

Common error: Distinguish a licence's permissions from the visibility of a file.

Question 4 · Command word: Explain2 teacher marks

A company sells an open-source program, while another gives away a closed-source utility. Explain why price alone does not distinguish open-source and proprietary licensing.

S7.05.A02S7.05.A04
Answer and checking points — teacher-written
  1. The sold program may retain modification and redistribution permissions, so a price does not remove its open-source status.
  2. The no-charge utility may still restrict changes and copying, so zero price does not grant open-source rights.

Common error: Compare permissions, not just payment.

Question 5 · Command word: State3 teacher marks

A proprietary licence covers five named users for one year, with support only during office hours. State three terms a manager should check before assigning it to eight users who need round-the-clock help for two years.

S7.05.A04
Answer and checking points — teacher-written
  1. Increase or change the permission to cover all eight users.
  2. Obtain a term or renewal arrangement covering two years.
  3. Arrange support availability that covers the required hours.

Common error: Use the stated licence constraints; do not assume payment covers every deployment.

Question 6 · Command word: Justify3 teacher marks

A volunteer group must modify a program and share it with partner groups. Offer A permits both acts if copyright notices are retained. Offer B permits use only and supplies no source. Justify one choice and state an obligation.

S7.05.A05
Answer and checking points — teacher-written
  1. Choose A because its modification rights allow the group's required changes.
  2. Its redistribution rights allow partner groups to receive the adapted copies.
  3. Keep the specified copyright notices when distributing the software.

Common error: A valid justification uses the offered terms rather than a category label alone.

Question 7 · Command word: Justify2 teacher marks

A shop is unsure whether a stock-control program works with its scanner. Its shareware trial includes scanner support for 30 days, and the shop can pay for continued use. Justify using the trial before purchase and explain one limitation.

S7.05.A03S7.05.A05
Answer and checking points — teacher-written
  1. Testing the supplied scanner support can establish compatibility before the shop pays.
  2. The trial only authorises the stated period, so a successful test still needs a licence purchase for later operation.

Common error: The scenario makes evaluation useful but does not establish indefinite free use.

Question 8 · Command word: Justify4 teacher marks

An archive needs software for twelve staff for three years. It must not modify source, but requires a support response within one working day. Offer A permits all installations at no charge with community help and no response commitment. Offer B covers twelve staff for three years and contracts the required response; its cost fits the budget and it forbids redistribution. Justify a choice and one trade-off. Later the archive must distribute an adapted version to partner archives: explain how that change affects the choice and one next step.

S7.05.A04S7.05.A05
Answer and checking points — teacher-written
  1. Choose B initially: its named-user term and contracted response meet the supplied requirements, whereas A does not promise the required response.
  2. The archive accepts B’s affordable cost and redistribution restriction in exchange for the required service; redistribution and source modification are not initially needed.
  3. The later adaptation/distribution requirement is not covered by B’s current permissions, so the original recommendation cannot simply be retained.
  4. Seek suitable changed permissions or compare another product, such as software with explicit modification/redistribution rights and a separate support agreement meeting the response requirement.

Common error: Do not infer modification rights from permission to install, or that community help guarantees a response time.

Question 9 · Command word: Explain2 teacher marks

A shareware editor stops saving documents after a 21-day trial unless a licence is bought. Explain one benefit of the trial and one action needed for continued full use.

S7.05.A03
Answer and checking points — teacher-written
  1. The user can test whether the editor suits their work before committing to a purchase.
  2. The user must obtain the stated paid permission to keep the full saving functionality after the trial.

Common error: Do not describe this time-limited offer as permanently free software.
