Hardware / 01

From a voice to stored samples, then back to sound

Look closely

What must change between air movement and a list of binary samples?

Imagine recording a short word and playing it back. The diagram shows a moving-coil microphone and a loudspeaker. Find the moving part at each end and predict what makes it move.

Step 1 / 5

Sound first moves the microphone

Sound is a variation in air pressure. It moves the microphone diaphragm. In this moving-coil example, a coil is attached to the diaphragm and sits in a permanent magnetic field. As the diaphragm moves, the coil moves with it. This is the first physical response to the voice.

Step 2 / 5

Movement becomes an electrical signal

The coil moves through the magnetic field and a varying voltage is induced. This analogue electrical signal follows the sound variations. A transducer converts one form of energy into another; here the microphone converts sound into an electrical signal. Other microphone designs use different physical mechanisms.

Step 3 / 5

Convert the signal into data

Recall sampling from Section 1. An analogue-to-digital converter, or ADC, measures the signal at regular instants, quantises each amplitude and encodes it in binary. The resulting sample values can be held in RAM and saved to secondary storage. A digital-output microphone can include this conversion inside its case.

Step 4 / 5

Prepare stored data for playback

The stored samples are numbers, so a digital-to-analogue converter, or DAC, turns the sample sequence into a varying analogue electrical signal. An amplifier supplies enough power to drive the speaker. The DAC prepares the signal; it does not itself move the air to make audible sound.

Step 5 / 5

Current now moves the speaker

Changing current through the speaker voice coil creates a changing magnetic field. Its interaction with the permanent magnet produces a force, moving the coil and attached cone. The cone moves the surrounding air, producing sound waves. Compare the direction of conversion: microphone motion produces a signal; speaker current produces motion.

Explain it yourself · Teacher-written check

A microphone diaphragm cannot move, but the ADC works. Why will changing the sampling rate not restore the recording?

Check your reasoning

The microphone cannot produce the normal electrical voice signal because the first physical conversion has failed. The ADC can sample its input, but cannot recover a voice signal that the microphone never supplied.

Apply the idea · Teacher-written practice
Question 1 · Command word: Explain5 teacher marks

Explain the difference between the moving-coil microphone and loudspeaker in a recording/playback system. Include what causes motion in each and where ADC and DAC are required.

S3.03.A03S3.03.A04
Answer and checking points — teacher-written
  1. Sound pressure moves the microphone diaphragm and its attached coil in a permanent magnetic field.
  2. That movement induces a varying analogue voltage.
  3. An ADC samples, quantises and encodes the input signal for digital processing.
  4. During playback, a DAC supplies an analogue signal from the stored sample values.
  5. After amplification, changing current in the loudspeaker coil produces force in the magnetic field, moving the cone and air.

Common error: A microphone’s mechanical motion produces the electrical signal; the loudspeaker’s electrical drive produces motion.

Keep this idea

Hardware / 02

Locate a block on a magnetic hard disk

Look closely

Why can reading a different block require both movement and waiting?

Find the circular platter, central spindle and arm carrying the read/write head. Predict how the head could reach data elsewhere on the surface: which part moves across the platter, and which part rotates? Later, use Explore to test your prediction one movement at a time.

Step 1 / 5

Locate the parts before the process

A platter is a disk with a magnetic surface. A motor turns the central spindle, rotating the platters. Data is organised in circular tracks, divided into sectors. A read/write head operates very close to the surface. The actuator moves its arm to reach a chosen track; platter rotation carries sectors past the head.

Step 2 / 5

Move to the required track

The controller identifies the location of the requested data. The actuator moves the head to the required track. This movement takes time, called seek time. Later, in Explore, request a different track and watch the head move before any block is transferred.

Step 3 / 5

Wait for the required sector

The correct track can be under the head while the required sector is elsewhere around the platter. Rotation must bring that sector to the head. This rotational waiting is separate from seeking. Even a read on the same track can wait if the sector has just passed the head.

Step 4 / 5

Distinguish writing from reading

During writing, the head changes the magnetic state of small regions on the surface to encode data. During reading, the head detects changes in the magnetic field from the recorded pattern. Circuitry decodes the resulting signal into binary data. Magnetic states retain the saved information without continuous power.

Step 5 / 5

Explain the delay using the mechanism

A disk needs powered electronics and moving parts to access data even though its saved pattern persists when switched off. The classroom model shows location and sequence; its animation speed is not a real disk benchmark. Describe seeking, rotation and transfer separately when explaining an access.

Explain it yourself · Teacher-written check

Check your reasoning

The required sector may not yet be beneath the head. The platter must rotate until that sector reaches the reading position; this is rotational waiting rather than head movement to another track.

Hardware / 03

Keep data in trapped electrical charge

Look closely

How does an unplugged USB flash drive keep a file without a spinning disk?

Compare the two cell states in the diagram. One contains trapped charge; the other does not. Imagine removing the external power, then reconnecting it before reading the cells.

Step 1 / 5

Replace the moving head with addressed cells

Flash storage contains many electronic memory cells. A controller selects cells by electronic addressing rather than by moving a head to a track. Solid-state means there are no mechanical moving parts involved in this storage process. It does not mean that the stored data can never change.

Step 2 / 5

Give a cell two distinguishable states

In the simplified floating-gate model, an insulating layer surrounds a region that can hold electrical charge. Programming places charge there; erasing removes it. The insulation allows the charge to remain after external power is removed. This retention makes flash non-volatile.

Step 3 / 5

Explain how the stored state can be read

The stored charge changes the threshold voltage at which the cell transistor conducts. A transistor acts as an electronically controlled switch; its threshold is the voltage needed to turn it on. Read circuitry tests the cell response and distinguishes a high threshold from a low threshold.

Step 4 / 5

Connect physical state and bit value

In the single-level example shown, the uncharged, lower-threshold state reads as 1 and the charged, higher-threshold state reads as 0. This is the declared encoding for the model. The controller decodes the sensed states into data. It still needs power to access a file even though power was not needed continuously to retain it.

Step 5 / 5

Follow an update

Flash can be erased and programmed electrically. Typical flash storage erases groups of cells, so changing a file may involve more than replacing an isolated bit. The controller manages these operations. The key distinction here is that stored charge persists, while read, erase and program operations need electrical power.

Explain it yourself · Teacher-written check

A flash drive retains a file while unplugged. Why does that not imply it can send the file to a computer without power?

Check your reasoning

The insulated cells retain their charge states without continuous external power. The controller and sensing circuitry need power to select cells, read their states and transfer the decoded data.

Hardware / 04

Read with light; write by changing a recording layer

Look closely

What must a writer change so that a reader can later recover the data?

Follow the optical path from laser to disc surface and back to the detector. Compare a low-power read with a higher-power write to a compatible recording layer.

Step 1 / 4

Follow the track with a laser

An optical disc rotates while an optical head positions a focused laser over its spiral track. The head moves across the disc to follow that track. The disc contains regions with distinguishable optical properties. A light detector measures changes in the reflected light, producing an electrical signal that the drive circuitry decodes into stored data.

Step 2 / 4

Separate a read from a write

Reading uses a low-power laser to detect an existing pattern without deliberately changing it. Writing uses enough laser energy to alter selected areas of a suitable recording layer. Those altered areas can later produce a distinguishable reflected-light signal. Reading and writing therefore have different effects on the medium.

Step 3 / 4

Check which kind of disc is present

A pressed read-only disc has a pattern formed during manufacture, commonly described using pits and lands. A recordable disc has a layer that the writer can change, for example by altering a dye. A rewritable disc uses a reversible material change. A writer cannot make every disc rewritable simply by increasing laser power.

Step 4 / 4

Compare three ways to keep the same file

A magnetic disk stores magnetic states; flash stores charge states; an optical disc stores an optically distinguishable pattern. Each retains saved data without continuous power, but each uses a different powered access mechanism. Explain a device using its own physical storage and reading process.

Explain it yourself · Teacher-written check

Why is “the laser cuts new pits into every disc when saving” an unreliable explanation?

Check your reasoning

It confuses a manufactured read-only pattern with recordable and rewritable media. Writing changes a compatible recording layer using laser energy; the layer and its update mechanism depend on the disc type.

Apply the idea · Teacher-written practice
Question 1 · Command word: Compare4 teacher marks

Compare writing and reading on a magnetic disk and a recordable optical disc. Explain why a pressed optical disc does not automatically support the same writing operation.

S3.03.A05S3.03.A07
Answer and checking points — teacher-written
  1. A magnetic write head changes magnetic states, while reading senses and decodes their pattern.
  2. A recordable optical writer changes a suitable recording layer using higher laser power.
  3. An optical read uses lower power to detect reflected-light differences without deliberately rewriting the layer.
  4. A pressed disc has a manufactured read-only pattern; its presence in a drive does not supply a compatible writable recording layer.

Common error: Do not transfer magnetic or recording-layer mechanisms to the wrong medium.

Keep this idea

Optical reading detects reflected-light differences; optical writing creates suitable differences in a compatible recording layer.

Hardware / 05

Build a page with charge, toner, heat and pressure

Look closely

The page looks correct, but its black powder rubs off. Which stage is missing?

Find the drum, laser, toner supply, paper path and fuser in the diagram. Follow one small dark area from its electronic page description to the finished sheet.

Step 1 / 5

Prepare an evenly charged drum

The printer converts the page description into a pattern to be produced. A photosensitive drum receives a uniform electrical charge. Photosensitive means its electrical behaviour changes when light reaches it. In the negative-charge example used here, the drum begins negatively charged.

Step 2 / 5

Use the laser to form an invisible image

The laser scans selected areas of the rotating drum. Exposure makes those areas conductive, reducing their negative charge while unexposed areas retain theirs. This creates a charge pattern corresponding to the page. The laser writes the pattern on the drum; it does not burn the image onto the paper.

Step 3 / 5

Make the pattern visible with toner

Toner is a fine powder. In this example negatively charged toner adheres to the exposed, lower-charge regions of the drum. The electrical conditions make toner collect where it is needed. This is a specific charge arrangement; changing charge signs without changing the rest of the explanation would give an inconsistent mechanism.

Step 4 / 5

Transfer the pattern onto paper

Paper passes close to the drum. A positive charge applied behind the paper attracts toner away from the drum and onto the sheet. The required image now appears on the paper, but loose toner has not yet been permanently attached. Transfer and permanent fixing are separate stages.

Step 5 / 5

Fix the image and prepare for another page

The paper passes through heated pressure rollers called the fuser. Heat and pressure bond the toner to the sheet. Remaining toner is cleaned from the drum and its charge is reset for reuse. If the image rubs off, investigate fusing even when laser exposure and transfer have worked.

Predict a result, change one condition, then explain what happened.

Explain it yourself · Teacher-written check

Explain why a working laser does not rule out a fuser fault.

Check your reasoning

The laser creates the electrostatic image on the drum. The fuser acts later, using heat and pressure to bond transferred toner to paper. Correct image formation can coexist with faulty permanent fixing.

Hardware / 06

Build the whole object one layer at a time

Look closely

If every layer has the right thickness, could the finished shape still be wrong?

The model has a broad bottom layer and smaller layers above it. Compare the three cross-sections with the completed object, then predict the result if the bottom slice is repeated at every height.

Step 1 / 4

Start with a digital three-dimensional model

Modelling or Computer Aided Design (CAD) software produces a digital model of the shape to be made. Slicing software divides that shape into thin horizontal cross-sections and produces instructions for forming each layer. A slice must describe where material is required at that height, not only how thick the layer should be.

Step 2 / 4

Form the first cross-section

In an extrusion example, a print head deposits softened material along the required path. That material forms the first layer. Other processes solidify material: a resin printer can cure selected liquid resin with light. Both approaches add material to build the object.

Step 3 / 4

Move and use the next slice

The head or build platform repositions for the next layer. The printer follows the next cross-section, depositing or solidifying material that bonds with the existing structure. It repeats the process at successive heights. The exact movement and material depend on the printer design.

Step 4 / 4

Check height and shape

The object is complete only when all required layers have been formed in order. Repeating the base slice can reach the correct height but make the wrong shape. A process that exposes an entire resin layer at once still works layer by layer; one layer is not a complete tall object.

Hardware / 07

Turn a touch into coordinates

Look closely

How does the computer distinguish touching Start from touching Stop?

In the diagram, locate the displayed button and the reported coordinate. Imagine the same image on a screen whose touch sensor is disconnected. Decide which functions could still work.

Step 1 / 5

Separate the image from the sensing layer

A touchscreen combines a display, which is an output device, with a touch-sensing layer, which is an input device. The display shows the button. The sensing layer provides information about contact. A visible image does not by itself prove that touch detection is working.

Step 2 / 5

Detect an electrical change

A capacitive touchscreen contains transparent conducting electrodes. A conducting finger near the surface changes the capacitance around the touched region. Capacitance describes the ability to store electrical charge. The screen controller detects this change; pushing harder is not the principal sensing mechanism.

Step 3 / 5

Calculate the location

The controller measures changes across the electrode grid. Their positions allow it to calculate touch coordinates, such as an x value and a y value. A coordinate identifies where the touch occurred. It does not itself mean Start, Stop or any other application command.

Step 4 / 5

Let software interpret the position

The application compares the coordinates with the bounds of its on-screen controls. A touch inside the Start button can request a start action. The software then changes the display or another output. The complete chain is electrical change → measured location → software interpretation → response.

Step 5 / 5

Recognise the resistive alternative

A resistive touchscreen has two conducting layers normally kept apart by spacers. Pressing the flexible upper layer brings them into contact. The controller uses voltage measurements in the two directions to calculate the contact coordinates. A suitable non-conducting stylus can supply the pressure. Distinguish this contact mechanism from the capacitance change sensed by a capacitive screen.

Explain it yourself · Teacher-written check

The image remains visible but no touches register. Explain how that is possible.

Check your reasoning

The display output can continue working while the touch-sensing input layer or coordinate processing has failed. They are separate parts of the input–processing–output chain.

Hardware / 08

Change the view when the head moves

Look closely

When you turn your head right, should the virtual room turn with you?

Compare the two gallery images. They show successive viewing directions from the same intended position, not the views of two eyes. Find the stationary cube and doorway in both images.

Step 1 / 5

Provide each eye with a view

An LCD-based headset uses liquid crystal display panels to present a separate image to each eye through lenses. The images show the virtual scene from slightly different viewpoints, helping create depth perception. The three-dimensional virtual world is represented by rendered images on the physical displays. Other headsets can use different display technologies.

Step 2 / 5

Measure head movement

A gyroscope detects angular movement as the head turns. An accelerometer measures acceleration and contributes information about movement and orientation. The tracking system combines sensor readings, with other tracking references where needed, to estimate how the user is viewing the world. The readings are movement input; they are not the displayed scene itself.

Step 3 / 5

Distinguish eye tracking from head tracking

Some headsets also use inward-facing eye-tracking cameras. They observe the eyes and provide images that software uses to estimate the gaze direction. The user can look towards a different object without turning their head. Eye-tracking data can support gaze-based selection or tell the renderer which part of the scene the user is looking at.

Step 4 / 5

Render the updated views

The computer calculates new left-eye and right-eye views for the updated viewpoint. When the user turns right, objects fixed in the world appear in different positions within the view. In the illustration the doorway moves towards the centre and the cube moves left; the room itself has not followed the head turn.

Step 5 / 5

Repeat with little delay

Tracking, processing, rendering and display updates repeat as the user moves. If tracking arrives late, the displayed view can correspond to an earlier head position. Two functioning displays are therefore insufficient: timely movement input and view updates are also needed for a responsive headset.

Explain it yourself · Teacher-written check

The headset shows sharp images, but the view stays fixed when the user turns. Which chain needs investigation?

Check your reasoning

The movement-tracking → viewpoint calculation → rendering update chain. A sharp image proves that output is visible, not that the view responds correctly to new movement input.

Apply the idea · Teacher-written practice

  4. Working displays can keep showing an old view if the tracking or rendering update fails.

Topic recap and preparation

Recall the binary and sound representation ideas from Section 1. Use basic charge, current and magnetic-field ideas to explain the devices here.

  • From a voice to stored samples, then back to sound: Recording needs sound → movement → analogue signal → binary samples. Playback reverses the information path using a DAC, amplifier and moving speaker.
  • Locate a block on a magnetic hard disk: Seek to the track, wait for the sector, then read or write the magnetic pattern.
  • Keep data in trapped electrical charge: Flash stores persistent charge states and reads them electronically, without seek or rotational delays.
  • Read with light; write by changing a recording layer: Optical reading detects reflected-light differences; optical writing creates suitable differences in a compatible recording layer.
  • Build a page with charge, toner, heat and pressure: Charge the drum → expose a pattern → attract toner → transfer to paper → fuse with heat and pressure.
  • Build the whole object one layer at a time: Slice the model, form a cross-section, reposition and repeat until every required layer is complete.
  • Turn a touch into coordinates: A capacitive controller detects an electrical change, locates it and sends coordinates for software to interpret.
  • Change the view when the head moves: Track movement, calculate the new viewpoint, render both eye views and update the displays repeatedly.
